# Rice Husk in Organic Farming: Best Uses (With or Without Urine), Ranked for Volcán

> **Literature review, Nov 2026 (second, deeper pass).** Every main way rice husk (*cascarilla*, *granza*) is used in organic agriculture worldwide, how well each works, where the studies disagree, and which uses fit our farm: Volcán Andisol at **pH 6.6–6.9** (our Sept 2026 tests), cool highland climate, and our inputs (husk, stored urine, compost, vermicast, wood ash, bone ash, biochar, Great White mycorrhizae).
> About 60 searches (Exa and web), with full texts read for the key papers (Schmidt 2017, Linam 2022, Williams 2023, Kammann 2015, Olanrewaju 2023, Omar 2021 and others).
> Companion file: [rice-husk-urine-silica-fertilizer.md](rice-husk-urine-silica-fertilizer.md) covers husk sealed with urine and husk silica chemistry. This file builds on it and corrects one point (§9.1).

---

## 1. Executive summary

1. **Raw husk is a carrier and structure material, not a fertilizer.** It is 15–20% silica and 20–25% lignin with C:N ~80–90, and it decomposes over years. Dug in raw at high rates, it ties up nitrogen for weeks; in a Japanese trial with vegetable soybean, growth, yield and taste all suffered. All of its value comes from what it carries (manure, urine, microbes) or what it is turned into (char, compost).
2. **Best use overall: char that has been "charged" with nutrients, placed in the root zone.** Charging means soaking the char in a nutrient liquid such as urine, or composting it.
   - In 13 Nepal field trials, cow-urine-charged char plus compost out-yielded the same urine plus compost by **+123% ± 77%** on average, using only 0.5–2 t/ha of char in the planting pits.
   - Independent trials show smaller but clear gains: +60% on cabbage and kohlrabi in Bangladesh, and +12% for maize in Indonesia vs urine alone. Human urine on pre-charged char gave okra +61% over urine alone (Nigeria).
   - One Malawi study found only *equal* yields vs urine alone.
   - **Never apply plain, uncharged char.** In a pot test, 2% plain char cut quinoa growth to 60% of the control, while 2% co-composted char gave up to 305% (Kammann 2015).
3. **Rice husk char is unusually rich for a char.** It carries P, K and Zn. In Timor-Leste, husk char at 20–30 t/ha raised vegetable yields by +183% at a neutral-to-alkaline site and +230% at an acid site, mainly by fixing P and Zn shortages. Our soil is already at pH 6.6–6.9, so expect the nutrient-carrier benefit, not much from liming.
4. **For silicon, raw or lightly charred husk beats ash.** At equal Si rates, raw husk raised soil-water Si by +45%, 450 °C char by +11% and >1000 °C ash by only +4% (Linam 2022). Short lab tests had favoured 500 °C char (Olanrewaju 2023), but the longer tests and plant studies favour raw husk. Burn it hot to ash and most of the silicon value is gone.
5. **Second best use: as the dry, airy part of compost, bokashi or poultry bedding.** Start at C:N 20–30, about 1 volume of husk to 1 volume of chicken manure. Urine can be the nitrogen source. Husk-heavy piles heat less and break down slower, so they need a hot nitrogen source and turning.
6. **Third: mulch, 5–10 cm deep.** It cut weeds by 77–91%, partly thanks to natural weed-inhibiting compounds in the husk (momilactones). It keeps soil moist and buffers temperature. It blows and washes away, and raw husk can carry rice and weed seed.
7. **Also good:**
   - 25–35% carbonized husk in seedling mix. Raw husk alone holds only ~9% water and stunts plants.
   - 10–40% husk in worm bedding.
   - Husk ash as a silicon source for corn (+17–19%). It is *not* a K source: ~0.5% K₂O, so use wood ash or SOP for potassium.
8. **Andisol phosphorus:** on a Java Andisol, rice husk ash at a very high 45 t/ha cut P retention and raised plant P uptake 2–3×. Pure silicic acid at lab doses did not. Silicon competes with phosphate for binding sites best at pH > 6, which is where our soil is. So expect a possible small P bonus, but keep bone ash and mycorrhizae as the P plan.
9. **Never mix stored urine with ash or fresh char in an open container.** At pH ~9 and above, its ammonium turns to ammonia gas and the nitrogen is lost. Charge char in a closed bucket and bury the mix promptly.

## 3. What raw husk is

| Property | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| C:N | 79–89 (leaves 34–41, bran lower) | Upland nitrate-immobilization incubation |
| Silica / lignin | 15–20% SiO₂ (amorphous opal); 20–25% lignin (13% in poultry-litter husk) | Companion file; Orrico et al. 2012 |
| Water retention as a substrate | **9% v/v raw, 10–13% burnt**, vs 35–50% for coir. Poor wicking: dry and soaked zones sit side by side for weeks | Calderón (Colombia), Dr Calderón Labs; UNAD review |
| Wettability | Only ~5% of raw particles sink in water vs ~100% of burnt husk | Calderón |
| Natural weed-inhibitors | Momilactone A 12–35 µg/g, B 8–38 µg/g (varies by rice variety) | Chung et al. 2006; Quan et al. 2019 |
| Other uses | Bedding, briquettes, mushroom supplement, hydroponic substrate | ECHO "Rice Hull Gold"; IRRI |

**Natural weed-inhibitors (momilactones).** These compounds inhibit weed germination: momilactone B fully stopped *Amaranthus*, *Cyperus* and *Leptochloa* at 20 ppm. They did not harm rice. Lettuce was relatively insensitive (IC₅₀ 327 µg/mL). This probably adds to husk mulch's weed control. A raw husk *seed mix* is a different story: pure raw husk germinated tomato at only 0–20%, though dryness explains part of that (Eviness & Nyirenda 2025). Whether composting breaks these compounds down is a gap.

### 3.1 Nitrogen tie-up from raw husk dug into soil

| Finding | Source |
|---|---|
| High-C:N residues tie up all the soil's mineral N within ~2 weeks, and this can last more than 10 weeks unless N is added | Residue N-mineralization modelling study |
| Incorporated rice and wheat residues: tie-up peaks around day 15, then N release begins. Residues left on the surface lower soil N slowly until day 75 | Rice/wheat residue incubation |
| Rice hulls in vegetable soybean (Japan): 8–22 mg N/kg tied up in year 2. At the high rate, growth, yield and taste suffered. Extra N fixed it | Japanese field study (CiNii) |
| Rice straw: incubating it 15–30 days before planting reduced the tie-up and improved growth | 1976 straw study |
| Raw husk dug into an Andosol paddy (20 t/ha) raised methane by +227%. Husk charcoal did not | Koyama et al. 2016 |

**Rule:** raw husk goes *on top* (mulch), *into a pile* (compost), or *into the root zone already loaded with N* (soaked in urine, 2–3 weeks before planting). Never dig it in plain right at planting.

## 4. Carbonized husk (biochar, *cascarilla carbonizada*, *kuntan*)

### 4.1 Making it

| Method | Notes | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Open chimney carbonizer (20 L can or drum with a chimney) | Light a fire, cover it with the can, mound 12–14 sacks of husk around it to chimney height (~1 m), and turn the mound. Quench with water when it is uniformly black, with no yellow (raw) and no grey (ash). A longer chimney is faster; a bigger mound risks ash | PhilRice "Farm carbonisation machine"; UM Research Journal (8 can designs) |
| Rice-husk gasifier stove (TLUD, Belonio type) | Cooks food and leaves char. Husk gave more char than coffee parchment, sawdust or cobs, which mostly burned to ash. Uninsulated stoves leave more char | ECHO AN32; Ethiopian Belonio stove study 2024 |
| Japanese commercial practice | 350–400 °C for 15 min; ~45% of the husk volume remains as char | Koyama et al. 2016 |
| Byproduct: husk vinegar (pyroligneous acid) | ~14 kg char + ~14 kg vinegar from 40 kg husk in a Taiwanese kiln. At dilutions around 1:200 it promoted growth in some trials; rice results were mixed | Tsukuba dissertation 2021; PeerJ 2024; Thai trial |

**Temperature trade-off:**
- **Short-test available Si** peaks around 500 °C for fine (≤1 mm) husk and around 600–700 °C for whole husk. It falls once silica crystallizes (Olanrewaju 2023).
- **Long-term Si release:** raw husk > 450 °C char > 600–950 °C char (Adamescu 2021).
- For a farm, 450–550 °C (fully black, still holding its shape) is the sensible middle ground.

### 4.2 Charging it: this decides whether it helps or hurts

| Finding | Source |
|---|---|
| **Nepal protocol:** quench the char with water (~60% moisture), mix 1:1 by volume with fresh cow urine, soak ≥1 h, mix with compost in a 30–40 cm pit (~35 cm wide) or furrow, and cover with 2–5 cm of topsoil so seeds and young roots don't touch it directly | Schmidt et al. 2017 |
| Charging glowing-hot char gave no advantage over charging cold, water-quenched char | Schmidt et al. 2017 |
| 1:1 urine charging loads ~60 kg N, 2 kg P₂O₅ and 66 kg K₂O per tonne of char | Schmidt et al. 2017 |
| Plain char at 2% cut quinoa growth to 60% of the control. Char co-composted with manure for weeks gave 139–305%. The co-composted char held nitrate (up to ~5,200 mg NO₃-N/kg), only partly extractable, and released it slowly | Kammann et al. 2015 |
| Why it works: composting forms an organic coating on the char's inner pores. The coating attracts water and holds N, K, Ca and Si | Hagemann et al. 2017 (*Nat. Commun.*) |
| Char added at 4% to manure compost captured up to 30% of the compost's nitrate | Hagemann et al. 2017 (PMC5310885) |

### 4.3 Yield evidence

| Trial | Result | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Nepal pumpkin, 8 farms, 0.75 t/ha char in pits | 82.6 t/ha. That is +306% vs urine + compost and +85% vs char + compost. Range across farms +116% to +742% | Schmidt et al. 2015 |
| Nepal, 21 trials, 13 crops | Urine-char + compost **+123% ± 77%** vs urine + compost (13 trials). It beat NPK-charged char by +103%. NPK-char gave +20% vs NPK alone | Schmidt et al. 2017 |
| Nepal peas (Udayapur) | Urine-char + manure gave 221% of the NPK yield and 292% of manure alone | Shrestha 2017 |
| Nepal bananas | +41% vs NPK; +102% vs compost alone | Pandit et al. (*J. For. Livelihood*) |
| Bangladesh cabbage and kohlrabi (75 farm plots) | 1:1 urine-char, ~2 L/m² (~2 t char/ha): **+60%** per area, heads ~50% heavier | Sutradhar et al. 2021 |
| Nigeria okra, *human* urine + palm-kernel char | Pre-charged char 211 g/plot; char and urine added separately 171; urine alone 131; control 55 | Yenagoa study 2026 |
| Indonesia maize, human urine | Char + urine 7.15 t/ha vs urine alone 6.05 (+18%) vs urea 5.11 | Vwalika et al. 2026 review |
| Malawi maize and soybean, human urine + cob char | Yields matched the fertilized control; urine alone did as well | Carpenter 2023 (thesis) |
| Bangladesh organic rice | Biochar-compost + cow urine was best: 4.03 t/ha | Tahura et al. 2025 |
| Timor-Leste vegetables, **rice husk char** 20–30 t/ha | Alkaline site: +183% average (chili 1.8 → 5.1 t/ha, carrots tripled). Acid site: +230% (tomato 4.5 → 13.9 t/ha; with P fertilizer 18.7). The char supplied P (561 mg/kg Mehlich-3), K and Zn, and raised soil pH from 4.6 to 6.3 at 20 t/ha | Williams et al. 2023 |
| Malaysia cucumber, husk char co-composted with chicken manure | 51 t/ha (10% char compost); char cut early N loss from 10.3% to ~0.4% | Manickam 2013 |
| Papua New Guinea sweet potato; Ghana lettuce (husk + cob char) | +20% (+100% with mineral fertilizer); lettuce +93% | Cited in Williams et al. 2023 |
| Andosol paddy, Japan, plain husk charcoal at 10–40 t/ha | No grain-yield gain over 3 years; more Si uptake and straw. N tie-up late in the season lowered grain weight | Koyama et al. 2016, 2017, 2019 |

**Meta-analyses:**
- Plain biochar: tropics about +25% at a median 15 t/ha; temperate ~0%. The effect falls as starting soil pH rises (Jeffery et al. 2017).
- Biochar only helped when combined with fertilizer (Ye et al. 2020).
- Greenhouse vegetables: +27%, not statistically significant (Gu et al. 2020).
- In acid soils the gains are largest below pH 4.5 (Plant & Soil meta-analysis).

**Where the studies agree:** charged, root-zone placement at low doses beats broadcasting a lot of plain char. The gain comes from delivering nutrients, not from liming.

**Where they disagree:**
- The very large Nepal effects come mostly from one research group, used woody feedstocks (*Eupatorium*) burned at 650–720 °C, and were run on silt loams with 1.5–5.4% organic C.
- Independent human-urine trials show +12% to +60%, or none (Malawi).
- On a fertile Andosol, plain husk char did nothing for grain.

**For our pH 6.6–6.9 Andisol:** expect a gain from charged char in planting holes, especially for heavy feeders. Don't expect liming. You already log a biochar + vermicast + urine input; the evidence supports keeping it, and charging it for at least an hour before it goes in the hole.

### 4.4 Ammonia loss: the main mistake to avoid

| Finding | Source |
|---|---|
| Ammonia gas loss rises with pH. Stored (already broken-down) urine at pH ~9 loses NH₃ quickly when spread on the surface or mixed with alkaline materials | Hung et al. 2021; general chemistry |
| *Fresh* urine dried in wood ash at pH >12 keeps up to ~90% of its N, because the urea doesn't break down. Real setups still lost 24–28% | Senecal & Vinnerås 2017; Senecal 2017 thesis |
| Wood-based char mixed into manure did not reduce ammonia loss over 21 days | Hung et al. 2021 |
| Char worked into the soil cut ammonia loss from urine patches by 45%, and the captured N stayed available to plants | Taghizadeh-Toosi 2011 |
| Wood ash applied 1–2 weeks before urine that was mixed into moist soil: no measurable ammonia loss | Neina & Dowuona 2013 |

**Practice:** our buckets hold *stored* (already broken-down) urine. Charge char with it in a **closed** bucket, bury the mix in the hole within a day, and never stir urine into wood ash or fresh husk ash.

## 5. Compost, bokashi and poultry bedding

| Finding | Source |
|---|---|
| Chicken manure + crushed husk: best at a starting C:N of 20 (over 50 °C for 7 days, germination index 91%, 60% moisture, pH ~8) | Gu 2015; Chinese thesis 2016 |
| Ghana: husk : chicken droppings 2:2 by volume gave 2.26% N, C:N < 20 | Murimi & Gbedemah 2023 |
| Husk + chicken slurry + feed + molasses 20:1:1:1, 60 days: N 1.15%, CEC ~101 cmol/kg, and the compost cut urea NH₃ loss | Omar et al. 2021 |
| Adding cow dung to husk compost: hotter (52.8 → 60.1 °C), less N loss, and 93–95% germination of Solanaceae vs 81% in peat | Zhang et al. 2025 |
| **Husk-bedded poultry litter heated the least** of six bedding types (lignin 13%); bagasse and Napier grass ran hottest and broke down most | Orrico et al. 2012 |
| India (ICAR): poultry litter + husk at C:N 25–35 → compost by day 98 → worm compost by day 116 | ICAR-DPR / CRIDA |
| Husk is good bedding (dries fast) but attracts rodents and wild birds in storage and carries more weed seed. Compost it before use | AgriFutures chicken-meat litter manual |
| *Trichoderma asperellum* inoculum (husk 55–65%, rice bran 15–25%, oil cake 15–25%, 60% moisture): pile over 60 °C on days 2–3, mature **5–7 days earlier**, slightly more N, P, Zn | Patent TW I287534B |
| *Trichoderma*-activated rice-residue compost inhibited *Sclerotium rolfsii* by 31–35% and slightly reduced cucumber damping-off | Lopez et al. 2015 |
| Urine as the compost N source: faster than urea (earlier 65 °C peak, +22% breakdown, +45% mineral N at 84 days) | Fatunbi & Mnkeni 2009 |
| Weekly cow urine on a manure-straw pile: N ×1.5, P ×2, shorter composting | Nguyen & Nguyen 2018 |

**Bokashi (*bocashi*) in Central America:**
- Husk is the aeration ingredient. Garita (MAG Costa Rica): 10 sacks husk : 10 chicken manure : 10 soil : 30 other manures and coffee pulp, plus charcoal, molasses, yeast and lime. Ready in 2–4 weeks.
- Classic recipe: 1 charcoal : 1 chicken manure : 1 husk : 1 rice bran : 3 soil + molasses (Ortega 2012, after Soto 2004). Doses: 10–30 g per leafy plant, 100–120 g per tomato or pepper.
- Honduras DICTA manual: 4 quintales husk : 4 manure : 4 soil, ready in 15 days.

**How bokashi compares with compost:** mixed results.
- Onion yield in Chiapas: 6.4 → 21 t/ha (Álvarez-Solís 2016).
- Vermont spinach: higher marketable yield at the second cut (SARE GNE15-098).
- Sweden: better than compost in only 1 of 16 comparisons (Norberg 2022).
- Read it as a fast, good compost, not a magic one.

## 6. Mulch

| Finding | Source |
|---|---|
| Tanzania (*B. carinata*): 5 cm cut weeds by 77%, 10 cm by 91%. 5 cm gave the best yield (+47% vs unweeded) | Amiri et al. 2025 |
| Indonesia (cucumber): −58% weeds; soil moisture 28.8 → 35.2%; mulches overall +78% marketable yield | Bengkulu trial 2018 |
| Nigeria (cucumber): coolest soil (27.4 vs 28.1 °C) and heaviest fruit | IOSR-JAVS |
| Nigeria (tomato): any mulch at 15 t/ha → 23.3 vs 8.1 t/ha; mulch type didn't matter | Tswanya 2017 |
| Nepal (cucumber): husk was the weakest mulch for weeds | Karki et al. 2020 |
| Nurseries: parboiled hulls ≥1.3–2.5 cm = 100% weed and liverwort control. Steaming kills seed; raw hulls are not sterilized | USDA-ARS (Altland); NC State |
| Temperature: organic mulch lowers *peak* soil temperature and keeps soil warmer on cool nights. Philippine highland vegetables benefited from the insulation | Castillo & Bacayan (BPI); Frontiers 2026 review |
| Problems: light enough to blow off; can surface or wash away in heavy rain; can shelter rodents; slug deterrence is only anecdotal | NC State; Applegater; KSU MF2900 |

**In Volcán:** use it on cool-season and shallow-rooted crops (cilantro, lettuce, onion) and in edged beds. Tuck it under a coarser mulch on slopes. For warm crops in the cool season, open the mulch until the soil warms.

## 8. Worm bedding and mushrooms

- **Worms:** cow manure with 10–40% husk gave more young *E. fetida* (824–844 vs 769), and the heaviest young at 40%. Castings fell as the husk share rose (Daegu Univ. 2006). Feed C:N ~25 was optimal (Mekelle Univ. thesis).
- **Mushrooms:** adding husk to rice-straw substrate for *Pleurotus* gave a quick, high yield at 100 g and the best quality at 200 g. Husk alone holds too little water for later flushes (ECHO). Spent substrate is best used as a compost feedstock.

## 9. Silicon from husk

### 9.1 Which husk form releases the most

| Finding | Source |
|---|---|
| At equal Si doses in rice pots, soil-water Si rose **+45% with raw husk, +11% with 450 °C char, +4% with >1000 °C ash**. Husk and char raised plant Si | Linam et al. 2022 (full text) |
| Long-term Si release: uncharred husk > 450 °C > 600–950 °C. Short (<5 day) lab tests underestimate raw husk and overestimate char | Adamescu et al. 2021 |
| Short CaCl₂ extraction: 500 °C fine char 2,562 vs raw 790 mg/kg; 300 °C char lower than raw | Olanrewaju et al. 2023 |
| Alkali-pretreated husk char made at 550 °C beat wollastonite and slag (rice yield +44–101%) | Wang et al. 2021 |

**Correction to the companion file:** it says low-temperature char and raw husk are better than high-temperature ash. That still holds. But *raw husk itself* is the strongest long-term Si source, and charring is optional for silicon. The reasons to char are N retention, P/K/Zn supply and avoiding N tie-up.

### 9.2 What silicon does for crops

| Effect | Evidence | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Corn yield | Husk ash 2.5 t/ha, 3 years: +17–19% | Haque et al. 2026 |
| Fall armyworm (corn) | Soil Si ≥600 kg/ha cut defoliation (no yield change). Foliar husk "biosilica" 2–3 L/ha, sprayed 3× from day 10, cut infestation and raised cob yield | Perdomo et al. 2022; Indonesian ICoSA 2021 |
| Powdery mildew (pumpkin) | Soil Si cut mildew and made fungicides work better | Rutgers (Heckman); *HortTechnology* 2019 |
| Organic cucumber | Si cut anthracnose but not downy mildew or nematodes | SARE LS06-187 |
| Tomato bacterial wilt (*Ralstonia*) | Pots: disease index −19% to −93%. Field (Ethiopia): only helped a moderately resistant cultivar. Si-modified 450 °C char: incidence −59% | Wang 2013; SCAU 2014; Ayana (JPPR); *Biochar* 2025 |
| Fusarium in shallot | Nano husk-ash foliar spray (0.3 g/L): severity −52% at 4 weeks; best combined with *Trichoderma* in the soil | IOP EES 1572 (2025) |
| Drought | Husk ash delayed potato senescence under water stress | Sharma et al. (ICAR-CPRI) |

### 9.3 On our Andisol

| Finding | Source |
|---|---|
| Japanese survey of 180 fields: Andosols are relatively *high* in available Si | Yanai et al. 2016 |
| **East Java Andisol:** husk ash at 44.6 t/ha cut P retention more than filter cake did, raised available P by +120%, and raised Chinese-cabbage P uptake ~3× in the high-organic-matter soil | Utami et al. 2012 |
| Andisol amorphous minerals adsorbed >98% of added P; silicic acid at 100 mg/L barely reduced it | Hanudin et al. 2014 |
| Silicon competes with phosphate for sorption sites only at pH > 6–7; no effect at pH 5 | Lee & Kim 2007; Haynes & Zhou 2018 |
| Meta-analysis of 15 studies: Si raised crop P uptake by +52% | Mückschel et al. 2025 |

**Reading:**
- Our pH of 6.6–6.9 sits in the range where silicon can free some phosphorus.
- The Java effect used a huge ash dose, and probably came partly from the ash's bases raising pH.
- Expect a modest P bonus from husk inputs, and keep bone ash plus mycorrhizae as the P plan.
- Biochar tends to raise soil mycorrhizal fungus abundance (global meta-analysis of 107 papers), which fits with Great White.

### 9.4 Liquid silica from husk ash (homemade potassium silicate)

- **Lab method:** husk ash + 6 M KOH (4 mL/g) at 70 °C for 6 h extracts ~98% of the silica (França et al., *J. Braz. Chem. Soc.*). A 10% NaOH version also works.
  - This is strongly caustic work, needing gloves, goggles and ventilation. It's not a casual farm brew.
- **Organic status:** the US organic program allows aqueous potassium silicate only when its silica comes from *naturally occurring sand*. A homemade husk-ash version wouldn't qualify there. Check whichever certifier you would sell under.
- **Simpler options:** compost the husk, or mulch with it, to supply silicon. Finely milled husk ash in water (~0.3 g/L) was used as a foliar spray in the Indonesian shallot trial, but it needs very fine milling.

## 11. Soil structure

- Husk dust at ~4.5 t/ha improved a heavy clay loam's bulk density, porosity and water flow (maize field trial).
- Husk char *reduced* infiltration on a sandy loam (2025 study).
- On Volcán's Andisol, which is naturally light, porous and well drained, husk adds little structurally. Its value here is as a nutrient carrier, a silicon source and mulch.

## 12. Urine with husk: ranked

1. **Best:** char the husk, soak it 1:1 in urine in a closed bucket for at least 1 hour (a few days is fine if sealed), mix with compost, place it in the planting hole, and cover with 2–5 cm of soil (§4.2).
2. **Next:** co-compost char or raw husk with urine and manure (§5). The char comes out pre-charged, the best form found in the trials.
3. **OK:** raw husk soaked in urine (our sealed buckets) is ammonium-charged husk. Use it as the N part of a compost pile, or put it in a trench 2–3 weeks before planting. It lost more N than char in a toilet trial (36% vs 19%).
4. **As cover material:** in dry toilets, husk char gave the best N retention and breakdown. Urine's ammonia helps kill pathogens (Global Water Pathogen Project).
5. **Don't:** mix stored urine with ash, or spread urine-soaked husk on the surface in the sun.

**Safety (WHO 2006 / EcoSanRes):** urine from your own household can go on your own crops. For produce you sell, store it ≥1 month first. Leave at least 1 month between applying it and harvesting.

---

## 13. Contaminants

- Husk biochar *lowered* cadmium in crops: wheat grain −31 to −39%; rice grain −13 to −25% (meta-analysis).
- Arsenic in rice grain was set by water management, not by the husk amendment (Linam 2022).
- No sources reported As or Cd levels in husk itself. Raw husk from commercial mills is generally considered low-risk, but this remains a gap.

---

## 14. Gaps

- No trial of husk products on vegetables in a *tropical highland Andisol*. The closest are the Java Andisol (Chinese cabbage, ash) and Japanese Andosol paddies.
- No head-to-head field test of raw husk + urine vs char + urine. The toilet N-loss study is the nearest.
- The large charged-char gains rest mostly on one group's Nepal trials with woody char. Human-urine + husk-char vegetable trials are few.
- Unknown whether composting breaks down husk momilactones, or how fast.
- No price for husk from Chiriquí mills. Agrosilos in David burns 40% of its husk for power, so ask mills directly.

---

## 15. Volcán action list

1. **Planting holes for heavy feeders** (squash, zucchini, tomato, corn, otoe): a handful to 0.5 L of 1:1 urine-charged husk char + compost per hole, covered with 2–5 cm of soil before planting.
2. **Compost:** layer husk, manure or kitchen scraps and urine (squeeze-test moist), add a few % char, and turn. Ready in about 6–10 weeks.
3. **Mulch:** 5 cm of husk on cilantro, lettuce and onion beds. Tuck it under coarser mulch on slopes.
4. **Seedling mix:** worm compost 50 : compost or bokashi 25 : carbonized husk 25.
5. **Corn and squash:** expect a small extra silicon effect, against fall armyworm and powdery mildew, from husk compost or mulch.
6. **Don't:** dig in raw husk at planting, apply plain char, or mix urine with ash.
