# Market Garden Beds: Size, Layout, First Preparation, Loose-Soil Depth (Volcán)

> November 2026. Worldwide literature review (about 50 searches; full texts of key papers and grower sources). Questions: best bed width (Perkins says 75 cm), path, length and height; how to prepare beds the first time; how deep the soil should be loose. Slope and contour layout is covered in [volcan-garden-bed-slope-design.md](volcan-garden-bed-slope-design.md); pH in [soil-ph.md](soil-ph.md).

## Recommendation for Volcán

> If the goal is maximum output per m² (fewest, narrowest paths), see [market-garden-space-maximizing-stones-sifting-volcan.md](market-garden-space-maximizing-stones-sifting-volcan.md): 1.0–1.2 m beds with 30 cm paths.

| Item | Choice | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Bed top width | **75 cm** | World market-garden standard. Hoops, netting, broadforks, seeders and rakes are made for it. You can step over it and reach the middle without bending far. |
| Path | **40–45 cm** (bed centres 1.15–1.20 m) | Room for a harvest crate and a foot. Paths also drain the heavy rain. |
| Length | **10 m, every bed the same** | Perkins' standard (7.5 m² per bed). One size of drip line, net and cover fits every bed. Shorter is fine on slopes, as long as all beds match. |
| Height | **10–15 cm**, raised to 20 cm in low or wet spots | Andisol drains well. Chiriquí growers use 15–20 cm in the wet season and half that in the dry season. |
| Loose depth | **About 30 cm loose, with no hard layer down to about 60 cm** | Test first. Loosen deeper only where a test finds a hard layer. |
| First prep | Test the soil, kill the grass with cardboard or a tarp, add 5–10 cm of low-nitrogen compost, mulch the paths | See step-by-step below |

**Choose 1.0–1.2 m beds instead** if you will mostly hand-plant large crops (cabbage, kale, peppers) and buy no 30-inch tools. Dowding, Cuba's organopónicos, IDIAP Chiriquí and AVRDC all use 1.0–1.2 m beds. You get about 20 % more growing area and fewer paths to weed (Frost). Whatever width you pick, use it for every bed. Bed width is the hardest thing to change later (Frost).

**Agreements**
- Use **one standard bed for the whole garden** (Fortier, Perkins, Frost, Crickmore, Coleman lineage). It is the biggest efficiency gain.
- Use **permanent beds and paths, and never walk on the beds**. Compaction stays in the paths.
- Width is set by **arm reach** (about 60 cm from each side is the most) and by **tools**.

**Disagreements**
- **75 cm vs 1.2 m.** 75 cm wins on tools, ergonomics and stepping over the bed. 1.2 m wins on growing area (about 20 % more land in beds) and fewer paths to keep weeded.
- **Stepping on beds.** Fortier: never. Dowding and Frost: occasional steps on a no-dig bed are fine, and even firm loose compost.

**Extra points for Volcán's humid climate**
- **Edge effect.** Outer rows yield more: carrots in outer rows were 1.5–2× heavier (Benjamin 1992), and border rows of peas and sweet potato gave about 40 % more (Anderson 1979; Camargo). On a 75 cm bed, 2 of 3–4 rows are edge rows. This makes up for part of the area lost to paths.
- **Airflow.** Narrower beds dry faster, which helps against mildew in 85–95 % humidity. This is an inference that matches the zucchini spacing doc, not a measured trial.
- **Hoops and nets.** 76-inch (1.93 m) wire hoops make arches about 66–76 cm wide and 40–45 cm tall, made for 30-inch beds. A net about 2.1 m wide (83 in) covers them. A 1.2 m bed needs a net 2.5–3 m wide.

**Ergonomics**
- Deep forward bending in field harvest produces about 3,000 N of lumbar compression, close to the NIOSH action limit of 3,400 N (Pinzke & Lavesson 2018).
- Comfortable reach is only 20–40 cm (Ohgama, Japan).
- Keep crates on the bed or in the path beside you; this halves deep bends.
- A low seated harvest cart cut "unacceptable postures" from 46 % to 0 % and made harvesting about 40 % faster (NIOSH).

## 2. Height

- Raised beds help when water would otherwise saturate the root zone:
  - Raised beds gave 10.6 % more carrot weight and 33 % lower penetration resistance on clay loam (Rohwer 2021).
  - Under monsoon waterlogging, onion yields were 40 % higher than on flat beds (ICAR 2025).
  - Radicchio: up to +30 % (Italy).
  - Cabbage did best on beds 30 cm high in a wet Indian plain (2026).
- With no waterlogging, extra height did nothing. Taiwan tomatoes on 40 cm vs 20 cm beds: no yield difference (Midmore 1997).
- Raised beds settle. Louisiana beds went from 15 cm to 6 cm in a year (Standifer 1975), and Fortier re-raises his beds every other year from the path soil.
- For Volcán's well-drained Andisol, **10–15 cm** is enough. Go higher only in hollows, in the tunnel's runoff zone, or for carrots. The tunnel doc already specifies 15–20 cm.

## 3. How deep should loose soil be?

**Roots go much deeper than any bed** (minirhizotron studies, Thorup-Kristensen and colleagues, Denmark):

| Crop | Root depth at harvest |
|---|---|
| Onion | 0.25–0.35 m |
| Leek | 0.5 m |
| Lettuce | 0.6 m |
| Sweet corn | 0.6 m |
| Potato | 0.7 m |
| Carrot | 1.1–1.6 m |
| Chinese cabbage | 1.3 m |
| Beetroot, summer squash | 1.9 m |
| White cabbage | more than 2.4 m |

**Roots slow down in hard soil, not merely un-dug soil.**
- Root growth halves at a penetration resistance of 0.8–2 MPa and nearly stops above about 2 MPa (Bengough 2011; Wolfe 1995).
- Roots that meet a hard layer bunch up above it.

**What a hard layer costs**
- A hard layer from about 10 cm down cut marketable yield by 73 % in direct-seeded cabbage, 49 % in snap bean, 41 % in cucumber and 34 % in sweet corn (Wolfe 1995, Cornell).
- Subsoil strength lowered vegetable dry matter by about 1 t/ha for every 0.5 MPa (Stone 1988).
- Trenching to 0.9 m raised yields of broad bean, cabbage, leek and beet on a compacted sandy clay loam (Stone 1988).
- Carrot yield fell 20–32 % on compacted soil (review by Riley 2014).

**Loosening deeper only helps where a hard layer exists**
- Double digging to 50 cm vs single digging to 25 cm vs only 6 cm made no difference in bean or beet yield on fertile, well-watered soil (Holt & Smith 1998).
- Double digging (45–60 cm) did win for broccoli on eroded, compacted ex-row-crop soil during a drought (Practical Farmers of Iowa 2021).
- In Kenya, double digging gave a small benefit that grew in the second season (Mbugwa 2006).
- California minimum-till: permanent beds tilled 20 cm vs subsoiled 50 cm gave about 5–10 % less lettuce; deeper tillage also reduced lettuce drop (Mitchell/Jackson).
- Cornell, 4 years of permanent beds: rototill 20 cm vs 10 cm vs no-till gave **the same yields**. Compost mulch drove the gains (Maher 2024).

**On Volcán's Andisol**
- Undisturbed Andisols are naturally loose: bulk density below 0.9 and often about 0.6 g/cm³, with 60–90 % pores (WRB). Highland topsoil is black and about 45 cm deep (MIDA). Roots get through easily.
- Old pasture, a trampled site or a plow pan can still be dense. A fallow Cartago Andisol measured 1.13 g/cm³.
- Tillage over years destroys particulate organic matter: −35 % after 5 years, −75 % after 10 (Antioquia, Colombia).
- Japanese organic no-till on Andosol: +65 % soil carbon and −17 % bulk density at the surface, with better water holding (Hashimi 2025).
- But Chinese cabbage under strict no-till on volcanic soil yielded less; light, shallow tillage matched full tillage (JIRCAS 2014).

**Rule**
- Aim for about **30 cm of loose soil**. That is a broadfork's depth (tines 25–40 cm), the Cuban 30 cm substrate, and the depth most Andisol topsoil already has.
- Check for a hard layer down to **60 cm**. Loosen deeper once only if you find one.
- After that, don't dig. Feed the surface and keep roots growing.

**How to test (moist soil, 2–3 days after rain)**
- **Wire flag:** push a stiff wire straight down. If it goes 30 cm easily, the soil is fine. If it stops at 10–30 cm, that is a hard layer.
- **Spade (VESS):** dig out a block of soil and look for platy layers, sharp-edged clods, or roots turning sideways (AHDB; SRUC).
- Watch for buried pumice stones (see below).

**Pumice stones in Volcán's soil**
- **Source.** The IDIAP onion manual for Tierras Altas (1994) says: *"Como ejemplo de suelos pedregosos tenemos los suelos planos del llano de Volcán, en los que la gran cantidad de pequeñas piedras pómez hace imposible el desarrollo normal de los bulbos y las raíces. Estos suelos requieren de la eliminación gradual de las piedras para que sean altamente productivos, labor que llevaría varios años."* In English: Volcán's flat land has so many small pumice stones that bulbs and roots cannot grow normally, and clearing them takes several years.
- **Why they are there.** The Volcán plain sits on Barú's huge debris-avalanche and lahar deposits. The Barriles deposit is about 9,000 years old and covers about 990 km²; the Caisán deposit is about 43,500 years old or more (Sherrod et al., USGS 2007; MTU studies). Volcanic gravel and pumice in the soil is normal here.
- **What they harm.** Only crops whose eaten part is underground. A carrot, onion or radish that hits a stone grows around it, so it forks, stubs or comes out misshapen (UC IPM; U. Maryland Extension). Stones and clods also increased carrot crown rot (AUSVEG). Pumice is otherwise harmless: porous, light and inert, and it helps drainage.
- **What to do.**
  - **Root and bulb beds** (carrot, onion, radish, beet, garlic): remove stones bigger than about 1 cm from the top 20–25 cm. Sieve the soil through a 1–2 cm mesh, or rake stones out after broadforking. The harrow tines on Bec Hellouin's Campagnole broadfork exist to lift stones. Keep these crops on the beds with the fewest stones.
  - **Leaf and fruit crops** (lettuce, kale, squash, tomato, beans): don't bother, beyond pulling stones larger than a fist.
  - **No-dig beds:** the 5–10 cm compost layer on top gives seedlings a stone-free start. Pick out stones each time a crop is pulled; the bed gets cleaner every year, as IDIAP says.
  - **Use what you remove:** pile it for path filler or drainage trenches, or screen out the fine pumice for seedling mix and container drainage.
- **Check your plot.** The IDIAP note is about the flat land of the Llano de Volcán. Dig 3–4 spade holes and count the stones before deciding how much clearing a bed needs.

## 4. First-time preparation, step by step

1. **Lay out.** Put beds on the contour on slopes (see the slope doc). Drive a stake at every corner so beds never drift (Crickmore). Group beds in blocks of the same size, with 0.6–1 m wheelbarrow lanes between blocks.
2. **Test the soil.** Check pH and P and lime if needed ([soil-ph-test-protocol.md](soil-ph-test-protocol.md)). Spread the amendments now, before anything covers the soil.
3. **Mow** the vegetation short.
4. **Loosen only if the wire test fails.** Broadfork once, rocking the soil without turning it over (Fortier: "especially the first year… when the biology is not really there"). Do it when the soil is moist, not wet; wet Andisol can smear and go fluid. Pull out roots and stones.
5. **Smother the vegetation**, choosing by weed type:
   - **Annual weeds or short grass:** one layer of plain cardboard, overlapped by 10–15 cm, under the beds **and the paths**, wetted. Perkins lays two layers under beds where grass has started growing. The cardboard rots in about 10 weeks; plant straight into the compost on top.
   - **Creeping grasses (kikuyu, star grass, Bermuda, couch):** cardboard alone fails; they regrow from rhizomes. Use a black silage tarp for at least 6–12 weeks. A Jamaica trial needed 6 or more weeks of black plastic to suppress grasses and nutgrass. Kikuyu needs 8–12 weeks of solarization, so pasture sod can take months. Or fork out the rhizomes by hand.
   - **Tarps in general:** 3 weeks kills emerged annual weeds (Cornell). Leaving the tarp on longer adds little for annuals (Maine). Tarping made 2.5 cm shallow tillage as good as rototilling (Rylander/Cornell).
6. **Compost layer.** Use 5–10 cm on cardboard (Dowding: 5 cm on clean ground, 7–15 cm on weedy ground; Perkins: about 10 cm of compost plus soil).
   - **Do not put 15 cm of rich manure compost** everywhere. 15 cm adds 3,600–10,000 kg N/ha, and nitrate builds up 60–90 cm deep (Ruch 2023). Compost mulch raised soil P by 497 % in 4 years (Maher 2024). Andisol tolerates P well, but nitrate leaching in 2,000+ mm of rain is real.
   - Prefer plant-based or woody compost. Or do what Perkins does: a thin layer of strong compost at the bottom with weed-free soil on top.
   - **Spread it evenly.** Perkins' "worst mistake": mixing two composts unevenly gave a year of patchy growth.
7. **Paths.** Cardboard, then wood chips level with the bed surface; top up as they settle. Cut the outer edge of the block straight down with an edging spade, then at 45°, and fill with chips; this keeps grass from creeping in (Perkins). No landscape fabric.
8. **First crops.** Plant shallow-rooted crops (lettuce, greens, radish) first, or punch holes through the cardboard for tomatoes and squash (Sattin). Plant right away; bare compost in the wet season washes away and grows weeds.
9. **Maintain.**
   - Add 2–5 cm of compost a year (Dowding 2.5 cm, Perkins about 5 cm). Expect to top up more often in Volcán; heat and moisture use organic matter fast.
   - Keep the soil always covered with a crop or mulch. Bare Andisol erodes in a downpour, and dried-out Andisol loses structure for good (slope doc).
   - Broadfork again only if a test or slow roots show compaction (Fortier now rarely needs to). Dowding stopped broadforking entirely on clay with good results.

## 5. Open questions and gaps

- No trial compares 75 cm vs 1.2 m beds for yield per total area including paths. The arguments are tools, ergonomics and path area, not measured yields.
- No tarp-duration data exists for highland tropical grasses (kikuyu, *Cynodon nlemfuensis*). Lift a corner of the tarp and check for live white rhizomes before removing it.
- No data on deep compost mulch in wet tropical highlands. Nitrogen and phosphorus losses are probably higher than in Germany or New York. Use thin, repeated layers.
- Living paths (*Arachis pintoi*, perennial peanut) need about 6 months to establish and compete at bed edges. No vegetable-path trial was found.
