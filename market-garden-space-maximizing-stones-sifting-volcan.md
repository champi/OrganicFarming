# Maximum Output per m²: Bed and Path Layout, Stones and Sifting (Volcán)

> November 2026. Worldwide literature review (about 38 searches, plus about 50 earlier ones for [market-garden-bed-size-setup-volcan.md](market-garden-bed-size-setup-volcan.md)). Goal: the most produce from the least land, with the fewest and smallest paths. Also covered: should stones be removed, and should soil be sifted?

## Summary

1. **Layout gains are real but capped.** Going from 75 cm beds with 45 cm paths (62.5 % of the land in beds) to 1.2 m beds with 30 cm paths (80 %) adds about **28 % more bed area**. That is the most geometry can give.
2. **Bigger gains come from what you do on the beds:**
   - Rain or tunnel cover: tomato +169 % and pepper +96 % (Belize). Bec Hellouin earned €80/m² under cover vs €38–48/m² outside.
   - Never leaving a bed empty: transplants, succession planting and relay planting. Bec's output rose 69–76 % in two years mainly from faster succession and intercropping.
   - Denser offset spacing: bean pods +14 % triangle vs square, +20 % square vs rows.
   - Intercropping: land equivalent ratio (LER) of 1.4–1.8, meaning 40–80 % more harvest from the same land.
   - Trellising vining crops: cucumber +24 % to +100 %.
3. **Recommended for Volcán:**
   - **Beds 1.0–1.2 m wide, paths 30 cm.** 77–80 % of the land is in beds.
   - One 60–80 cm wheelbarrow lane per block, not between every bed.
   - Mulched paths (wood chips or cardboard). Living paths cut yields in trials.
   - Prefer 1.0 m beds if reaching 60 cm into the bed strains your back. Use 1.2 m in the tunnel, where Dowding uses 1.3 m beds with 30 cm paths.
4. **Stones:**
   - Leave small stones and pumice in most beds. They are neutral or even helpful: pumice is a porous amendment, and stones hold big pores open and protect the soil from compaction.
   - Remove stones from the **top 20–25 cm of root and bulb beds** (carrot, parsnip, radish, onion, garlic, beet). In a Canadian test, a stone-burying bed former raised carrot and parsnip yields by 30 % or more.
   - Pick out stones bigger than a fist anywhere. Don't strip stones from the surface of slopes: in one study, removing stones raised runoff 1.4–1.7× and soil loss 1.5×.
5. **Sifting:**
   - **Don't sift whole beds.** Sieving pulverizes soil structure, and the soil then slumps and crusts. Andisol's loose macrostructure is its weak point.
   - Sift only the **top 3–5 cm of direct-seeded rows**, or better, cover the seed with **sifted compost**. The ideal seedbed is crumbs of 1–5 mm. Crumbs under 1 mm crust over and rot seed; crumbs over 10 mm cut emergence.

## 1. Geometry: how much of the land is growing area

Share of land in beds = bed ÷ (bed + path). Cross lanes are not included.

| Bed | Path | Bed centres | % of land in beds | Who uses it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 75 cm | 45 cm | 1.20 m | 62.5 % | Fortier |
| 75 cm | 35 cm | 1.10 m | 68 % | Crickmore, Perkins (30–35 cm) |
| 75 cm | 30 cm | 1.05 m | 71 % | Sattin, Frost (tunnels) |
| 80 cm | 20 cm | 1.00 m | **80 %** | French microfarm trial in open field (MIPS Auvergne) |
| 1.0 m | 30 cm | 1.30 m | 77 % | Cerro Punta onion growers (IDIAP: 1.1–1.2 m beds, 30–40 cm paths) |
| 1.2 m | 30 cm | 1.50 m | **80 %** | Dowding (30 cm paths) |
| 1.2 m | 50 cm | 1.70 m | 71 % | Cuban organopónicos |
| 1.3 m | 30 cm | 1.60 m | 81 % | Dowding's tunnel centre beds |

- **Cross lanes:** with 10 m beds, each 60 cm cross lane costs about 6 % of the land. Longer beds and fewer lanes lose less.
- **Measured farm shares:**
  - Bec Hellouin's studied area was 1,000 m² of beds within 1,500 m² including paths, so 67 % beds.
  - French permanent-bed farms average about 60 % (PimAB).
  - The MIPS trial measured 80 % in the open field and 60–62.5 % under cover, where paths were wider.

### Does a wider bed give more yield per m² of bed?

- **No clear effect.** Drip-irrigated vegetables on beds 41, 61 and 81 cm wide gave the same yield; only squash in one year rose with width (Clark & Maynard 1992, Florida).
- **Sometimes the narrower bed wins.** Pole beans under a net house with 30 cm furrows gave the **highest yield per acre on 75 cm beds**, ahead of 105 cm and 37.5 cm beds (PAU 2025). Onion did best on 45 cm beds with 30 cm furrows (Ethiopia 2026).
- **Why:** the number of rows that fits a bed, plus the **edge effect**, matter as much as the share of land in beds. Outer rows yield more: carrots 1.5–2× heavier (Benjamin 1992), peas and sweet potato about +40 %. Narrow beds have more edge.
- **Practical rule:** choose the bed width so that a whole number of crop rows fits. RECA (Niger) notes growers narrow cabbage beds to 0.7 m because only 2 rows at 40 cm fit anyway. Cutting paths from 45 to 30 cm gives a certain gain. Widening beds only pays if it adds rows.

### How narrow can paths be?

- **30 cm is the practical minimum** for reaching in, standing and setting down a crate (Dowding, Sattin, Frost in tunnels). Dowding: "just wide enough for access to harvest leaves."
- 20 cm works only in the open where nothing is carried along the path (MIPS). Crickmore uses 35 cm to fit 13-inch crates and a 12-inch wheel hoe.
- **Drop wooden sides.** They take up space and harbour slugs (Dowding).
- **Plant the paths.** Without sides, crop roots grow into mulched path soil (Dowding). Some French growers plant lettuce or lamb's lettuce in every second path (PimAB).
- **Living paths cost yield.**
  - White clover or ryegrass paths cut yields of tomato, cabbage and lettuce (Pieper 2015).
  - Clover paths cut yields of broccoli, cauliflower and pepper (SDSU 2022–24).
  - Clover paths gave less zucchini than straw mulch (Bruce 2022).
  - Mulch paths with wood chips, cardboard or straw instead.

### Why not drop the paths and walk on the beds?

- Most compaction from footsteps happens in the first few passes.
  - Desert soil: about 10 passes compacted it significantly; bulk density rose from 1.30 to 1.51 g/cm³ after 100 passes (Lei 2004). One vehicle pass equals about 10 footsteps.
  - Trail soils: +0.3 g/cm³ in the top 0–5 cm (St James Walkway).
- Walking on beds therefore throws away the benefit of beds. If you must step in, use a board to spread your weight and stay off wet soil.
- Dowding steps on no-dig beds occasionally. Fortier never does.

**Humid-climate limit on density.** Close spacing raises disease losses:
- Mini lettuce: more leaves discarded at high density (Takahashi).
- Lettuce soft rot: 3 % at close spacing vs none at wide spacing.

In Volcán, use the dense end only with good airflow, under cover, or in the dry season (see the zucchini spacing doc).

## 3. Stones: remove or not?

**General effect of stones in topsoil (research)**
- Stones can help:
  - Small stones (under 5 cm) protect soil from erosion and keep moisture. Ethiopian farmers keep them on purpose. Removing them raised soil loss, and the effect on yield was weak (Nyssen 2001).
  - Removing all surface stones cut rainfed wheat yield by 20 % (Danalatos 1995, Greece).
  - With 15–20 % or more gravel, the gravel acts as a skeleton that protects the soil from compaction and keeps big pores open (Priori 2020).
- Stones also take up space:
  - Mixing 10–30 % coarse fragments (10–19 mm) into potato soil raised bulk density and cut water held at field capacity from 21 % to 8 %. There was **no yield or tuber-quality difference** over 3 years (Chow 2007, Canada).
  - Removing or crushing stones raised runoff 1.4–3× and soil loss 1.5–2× (Chow 1992).

**Pumice specifically (Volcán)**
- Pumice is porous and holds water inside the stone.
- Adding 30–45 % pumice (4–8 mm) to soil improved water retention and big pores, and improved strawberry growth (Turkey).
- Sieved pumice gave the highest tomato yields among substrates (Mexico).
- In beds of leaf and fruit crops, small pumice works as **a free soil amendment**, not a defect.

**Root crops are the exception**
- Stones block roots, so carrots and parsnips fork and bulbs deform (UC IPM; AAFC).
- A stone-burying bed former, which puts stones about 30 cm down, gave **30 % or more extra carrot and parsnip yield**, straighter roots and less storage damage. The farmer had lost 30–40 % of root-crop yield to stones (AAFC Newfoundland 2024).
- Japanese potato stone and clod separation cut misshapen tubers from 6.0 % to 1.3–1.8 %, with the same total yield, and cut harvest labour by 28 % (Ichiki 2013).
- IDIAP warns specifically that the many small pumice stones on Volcán's flat land stop bulbs and roots from developing normally.

**Rule for Volcán**

| Bed use | Action |
|---|---|
| Carrot, parsnip, radish, onion, garlic, beet, ginger | Screen the **top 20–25 cm** through a **1–1.3 cm (½ in) mesh** once when you build the bed. Pick out stones each time you harvest. Keep these crops on these beds. |
| Leaf, brassica and fruit crops, beans, squash | Leave stones under about 5 cm. Remove fist-size stones that get in the way of tools and planting. |
| Seeder rows | The Jang seeder tolerates some stones. Coleman 4-row and 6-row seeders need a stone-free bed, and the multi-row Jang JP6 needs a "perfectly prepared" one (Terrateck). |
| Slopes and bed edges | Don't strip surface stones. Use removed stones in paths, drains and edges. |

The 1–1.3 cm mesh is a practical choice: the ½ in mesh is what growers report using. No study tests mesh size for carrots.

## 4. Sifting soil

- **Sifting whole beds harms structure.**
  - Potato destoners that sieve soil "can create a lack of structure causing ridges to slump," and can compact the layer underneath. Fewer passes are better (Stalham, British Potato Council review).
  - Extension advice agrees: "Do not overwork or pulverize the seedbed… promotes crusting and erosion" (Virginia Tech). Leave marble-size pieces (Utah State).
  - Andisols have a weak, fragile macrostructure on top of very stable microaggregates (WRB lecture notes), so sieving destroys the part that drains the soil and lets air in.
- **What a seedbed needs.**
  - Emergence is best when seed sits on a firm moist layer, covered by soil mostly finer than 5 mm. Crumbs under 2 mm and 2–5 mm worked equally well (Håkansson 2011, 2013).
  - Emergence was very poor under 10–25 mm clods (Håkansson).
  - Under 1 mm the surface crusted and seed rotted (Rathore).
  - The optimum is 1–5 mm crumbs, about 50 % finer than 6 mm, over a firm base (Braunack 1995; Berntsen; Nasr & Selles).
- **Practice:** for direct-sown small seed (carrot, radish, lettuce, salad mix):
  - Rake the top 3–5 cm.
  - Or lay a 1–2 cm band of **sifted compost** in the seed row.
  - Firm the row lightly after sowing.
  - Transplanted crops need no fine tilth at all.

## 5. Paths, wheelbarrows and bed sides

**Block layout**
- Narrow 30 cm paths between beds are for feet, kneeling and crates. One wide 60–80 cm wheelbarrow lane runs only along the bed ends of each block of 8–12 beds.
- Park the wheelbarrow in the lane and carry loads the last few metres. With 10 m beds and a lane at each end, nothing is more than 5 m from a lane. Fortier, Cuba's organopónicos and the French microfarms use this layout.

**Wheelbarrows in 30 cm paths**
- The wheel (about 8–10 cm wide) fits a 30 cm path. The tray and handles of a typical wheelbarrow are about 60–70 cm wide, so they stick out over the beds. These are typical sizes, not sourced figures.
  - Empty beds or low crops: you can push a wheelbarrow down the path, for example to spread compost when preparing beds.
  - Tall or bushy crops (tomato, kale, beans, zucchini): the barrow breaks plants, and your feet step on the bed edges.
- Inside narrow paths, use crates (about 33 cm wide; Crickmore sized his paths to 13-inch crates), buckets, or a narrow 30–40 cm garden trolley.

**No wooden bed sides**
- Boards take up 3–5 cm on each side of every bed. Without them, crop roots grow into the mulched path soil (Dowding).
- Rotting wood harbours slugs and woodlice, which is worse in humid Volcán. Dowding strongly advises against boards in tunnels.
- Instead, the bed is a mound 10–15 cm high with sloping edges. The condition: paths must stay weed-free, or grass creeps into the bed.

## 6. Best layout summary (most food per m²)

- **Beds:** 1.0–1.2 m wide (1.2–1.3 m in the tunnel), 10 m long, all the same size. Mounded 10–15 cm high, no wooden sides, stakes at the corners, on the contour on slopes.
- **Paths:** 30 cm, mulched with wood chips or cardboard (not clover or grass). One 60–80 cm wheelbarrow lane per block of 8–12 beds.
- **Result:** 77–80 % of the land is growing area, against 62.5 % for 75 cm beds with 45 cm paths.
- **Soil:** about 30 cm loose (wire-flag test; broadfork once only if you hit a hard layer). Screen stones from root and bulb beds only. Never sift whole beds; make a fine seedbed only for direct sowing. Spread 5–10 cm of low-nitrogen compost at the start, then 2–5 cm a year.
- **Production levers, in order:**
  1. Rain cover or tunnel (+50–170 %) — see [rain-covers-bed-shelters-volcan.md](rain-covers-bed-shelters-volcan.md)
  2. Never leave a bed empty (transplants, multisowing)
  3. Intercropping and relay planting (40–80 % more from the same land)
  4. Trellising (+25–100 %)
  5. Offset (triangle) spacing at the dense end, eased in humid spells
  6. Fast crops (salad mix, radish, rocket, cilantro)
- The layout adds up to about 28 % more growing area. Levers 1–3 can roughly double the harvest from the same ground.

## 7. Recommended Volcán layout for the most food per m²

1. **Beds:** 1.2 m (tunnel 1.2–1.3 m) or 1.0 m, with **30 cm mulched paths**, no wooden sides, and corner stakes. 77–81 % of the land is in beds.
2. **Blocks:** 8–12 beds, 10 m long or longer. One 60–80 cm wheelbarrow lane per block.
3. **Root-crop beds:** screen stones from them once. Leave stones elsewhere. Never sift whole beds.
4. **Cover:** put the highest-value and most rain-sensitive crops (tomato, pepper, lettuce, cucumber) under the tunnel or rain covers ([volcan-tunnel-design.md](volcan-tunnel-design.md)).
5. **Keep beds full:** raise transplants and multisown modules while the current crop finishes, and replant the same day a bed clears.
6. **Spacing:** offset (triangle) planting at the dense end of the range, moderated for humidity.
7. **Trellis:** climb cucumbers, pole beans and tomatoes. Underplant short crops at the base or on the sunny side (relay or intercrop).
8. **Hoops and nets:** a 1.2 m bed needs roughly 2.5–3 m wide netting and longer hoops than the standard 76-inch (75 cm-bed) ones; that is the cost of wider beds.

## Gaps

- No trial measures yield per **total** area (paths included) for 75 cm vs 1.2 m beds in mixed hand-worked vegetables. The gain above is geometry plus the edge-effect evidence.
- No mesh-size study exists for carrots. No study measures how pumice size affects carrot forking.
- The humid-tropics density limits come from lettuce disease data. Optimal dense spacing in Volcán needs your own trials.
