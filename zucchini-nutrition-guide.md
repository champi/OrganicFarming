# Zucchini (Cucurbita pepo) Nutrition — Organic, Volcán Andisol

> **Volcán, Chiriquí, Panama.** ~1,400 m, 14–24 °C (mean ~19 °C), 2,400–3,600 mm rain/yr. Andisol, pH ~6.6, P retention up to 83%.
> **Inputs on hand:** aged and fresh urine, Epsom salt, biochar, vermicast, bone ash + citric acid, wood ash, Great White mycorrhizae.

## TL;DR

Per plant (~1 m² each), over one ~3-month crop:

| Nutrient | Crop takes up | What to apply | Source | When |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **N** | 14 g | 3.5–7 g N as side-dress | **Aged urine**, 100–200 mL/week (≈1–1.5 L/season) | Weekly from ~3 weeks after sowing |
| **K** | 9.3 g | ~8 g K₂O beyond what urine gives | **Potassium sulfate (SOP)** ~5 g × 3, *or* SOP + a little wood ash | First flowers → after first harvest, split |
| **Ca** | 8.0 g | ~8 g Ca, mostly already in soil + vermicast | Bone ash (~37% Ca) + soil; gypsum only if symptoms | With the P |
| **P** | **0.9 g** | ~2–3 g P (≈ 15 g bone ash) | **Lightly** acidified (6–8% citric) or raw bone ash in vermicast, so the **mycorrhizae** do the solubilizing | **Once**, ≥4 weeks after inoculation, before ~40 DAS, at the root-zone edge |
| **Mg** | 2.5 g | ≤0.8 g Mg | Epsom already in the urine (≤5 g/L). It does **not** threaten Ca at this rate (§5). | With urine |

**The corrections from earlier drafts:**
1. **P dose:** applying 3–8× the crop's P uptake is normal on a P-fixing Andisol, but **45 g of bone ash per plant was too much**. With mycorrhizae (Great White) in play, about **15 g**, applied as *slow* P, is the better target (§3, §7).
2. **K source:** **wood ash cannot cover zucchini's K need** at your pH 6.6. The amount needed would add roughly a tonne/ha of lime equivalent. Use potassium sulfate as the main K source; wood ash is optional and only a small top-up.
3. **Don't fully dissolve the bone ash when you rely on AMF.** Full citric-acid treatment turns bone ash into *soluble* P, which is exactly what suppresses mycorrhizae. With AMF present, raw bone char gave maize as much P as superphosphate on a P-fixing soil (Zwetsloot et al. 2016). Let the fungi do the dissolving (§7).

## 1. When the Plant Needs What

Data: **Purquerio et al. 2019**, Corona F1 Italian zucchini, São Paulo, 20.6 °C mean (13.9–27.4 °C), a near-match for Volcán.

| Nutrient | Total per plant (84 days after transplant) | Peak daily uptake | Main uptake window (days after transplant) |
|---|---|---|---|
| N | 14.2 g | 0.36 g/day at day 45 | 24–64 |
| K | 9.3 g | 0.34 g/day at day 40 | 26–53 |
| Ca | 8.0 g | 0.33 g/day at day 41 | 29–53 |
| Mg | 2.5 g | — | — |
| S | 1.2 g | — | — |
| P | 0.9 g | 0.03 g/day at day 41 | 23–57 |

Growth is slow until ~27 days after transplant, then 79% of the dry mass is built between days 27 and 67. **Fruit removes more K than anything else**, in the order K > N > P > Ca > Mg > S (Tavares 2023).

**Converting to days after sowing (DAS) in Volcán:** transplants are ~2–3 weeks old, so add ~17 days. Volcán's ~1.5 °C cooler mean slows development by roughly 10–15%.

| Phase | DAS |
|---|---|
| Slow establishment | 0–40 |
| P window opens | ~40 |
| First harvest | ~50–60 |
| Peak N/K/Ca uptake | ~60–68 |
| Main N window closes | ~85–90 |

**Consequence:** P has to be placed **before ~40 DAS**, because it barely moves in soil. N and K are needed steadily, peaking 60–70 DAS.

## 2. Nitrogen — Urine

**Rate.** In no-till zucchini, a side-dress of **34–67 kg N/ha** at 8–12 true leaves gave the most fruit. 134–201 kg N/ha grew bigger plants with *less* fruit (Rich et al. 2012). UGA recommends 80–120 lb N/acre in total: half pre-plant, then two equal side-dresses, at 8–10" tall and after first harvest.

| | Per plant |
|---|---|
| Side-dress target | 3.5–7 g N |
| Aged urine (Rich Earth analysis 0.57% N) | ~5.7 g N/L |
| Season total | **0.6–1.2 L, up to 1.5 L** (SSWM rule: 1.5 L/m²/season) |
| Dose | 100–150 mL early → 200 mL at peak → 100–150 mL late |
| Dilution | 1:4–1:5 (Rich Earth found no yield difference between undiluted and 1:3) |
| Frequency | **Weekly** (small and frequent retains more N; diluted urine can go on up to twice a week) |

**How:** pour (don't spray) on moist soil 15–20 cm from the stem, then water in or cover with mulch. Stored urine left on the surface lost **up to 34% of its N** as ammonia (Nutr. Cycl. Agroecosyst. 2023).

### Aged + Epsom vs. fresh + vinegar

| | Fresh + vinegar (0.5–1 L vinegar per 20 L jug) | Aged + ≤5 g/L Epsom |
|---|---|---|
| N form | Urea, stable for weeks | NH₃/NH₄⁺, pH ~9 |
| N loss | Low while pouring; soil urease converts the urea within ~1–3 days anyway | Higher if left on the surface; small if watered in |
| **Pathogens** | **Not sanitized.** The acid blocks the high-pH/ammonia kill. | **Self-sanitizes** in storage (WHO; Rich Earth) |
| WHO rule (own household) | ≥1 month between application and harvest | ≥1 month stored: safe at household level; ≥6 months: all crops |
| P | Stays soluble, then fixed by the Andisol | **Struvite** forms (slow-release P that resists fixation). Shake the jug before measuring. |

**Rule:** use **fresh + vinegar** only while harvest is >1 month away, or for biochar charging and compost. Once the first fruit is <1 month away, use **aged urine only**. Rich Earth uses vinegar for odor control and for concentrating urine, and still pasteurizes everything before it goes to farms.

**Epsom ≤5 g/L.** 1.5 L × 5 g/L = 7.5 g Epsom ≈ 0.74 g Mg, below the crop's 2.5 g Mg. Do not raise it: Mg²⁺ competes with K, which is zucchini's #2 nutrient.

## 3. Phosphorus — "Only 0.9 g?" Then Why Apply Several Grams?

### The plant takes 0.9 g; the soil takes most of what you add

| Fact | Number | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Zucchini P uptake | **0.9 g P per plant** (5.3 kg P/ha exported in fruit) | Purquerio 2019 |
| Applied P retained by a Costa Rican Andisol | **93%** year 1, 91% year 2 | Agronomía Costarricense 2008 (Juan Viñas potato) |
| Residual value of P on that Andisol | **None** when applied to the first crop only. Apply every crop. | same |
| P retention, Chiriquí volcanic soils | up to 83% | Morán et al. 2022 |
| First-year crop recovery of applied P (general) | ~10–30% | 4R/agronomy literature |
| UGA squash P rec. (Low / Medium / High soil test) | 120 / 80 / 40 lb P₂O₅/acre ≈ **134 / 90 / 45 kg P₂O₅/ha** | UGA C527 |
| Andisol potato trial range | 150–600 kg P₂O₅/ha | Agronomía Costarricense 2008 |

**So the answer is fixation.** If the crop recovers only ~10–30% of what you apply, delivering 0.9 g to the plant takes ~3–9 g P in the soil. That is exactly the range of the extension rates: **90 kg P₂O₅/ha = 9 g P₂O₅/m² ≈ 3.9 g P per plant**.

### Where "45 g bone ash per plant" came from, and why it's too high

- It was the bone-ash guide's generic rate for fruiting vegetables: **300–600 kg bone ash/ha = 30–60 g/m²**.
- Bone ash is hydroxyapatite. **Pure hydroxyapatite is at most 18.5% P / 39.9% Ca**, so real bone ash is **~13–18% P (30–42% P₂O₅)**. The "34–42% P" figures in the bone-ash guide's table must be P₂O₅ or surface-scan values (EDX). Read them as P₂O₅.
- 45 g × ~16% P ≈ **7 g P ≈ 16 g P₂O₅/m² ≈ 165 kg P₂O₅/ha**. That is above UGA's *low-P-soil* rate, on a plant that already received struvite-charged biochar + vermicast, and will get ~0.5–0.7 g P from 1.5 L of urine.
- The Andisol shows **no residual effect**, so a big dose doesn't "bank" P for next season. Most of it gets fixed.
- **High soluble P suppresses mycorrhizal colonization** (shown in corn, cilantro and tomato; petunia shows the mechanism). That works against the AMF seed coating.

### Corrected dose

| Your soil P status (unknown; no P test yet) | Target | Bone ash per plant (at ~16% P) |
|---|---|---|
| **Medium, with active mycorrhizae (default)** | ~50–60 kg P₂O₅/ha ≈ 2–3 g P | **~15 g**, applied as slow P (§7) |
| Medium, no AMF | 90 kg P₂O₅/ha ≈ 3.9 g P | ~20–25 g |
| Low (if a test shows it) | 134 kg P₂O₅/ha ≈ 5.9 g P | ~35 g |
| High | 45 kg P₂O₅/ha | ~10 g, or skip |

**Repeat crops in the same bed:** bone P keeps releasing for 2–3 years (~5–28% in year 1, ~63% by year 2, 69–87% by year 3; Ylivainio 2008 via Nogalska 2022), so use **about half the dose (~7–8 g)** on the next crop, and soil-test P (Olsen or Mehlich-3) every 1–2 years. Skip bone ash for a cycle if P tests medium–high. In a six-year trial, dosing well above crop removal (68–90 kg P/ha/yr vs 45) dropped P use from 36% to 15–21% and left 189–328 kg P/ha unused in the soil (Nogalska 2022).

**Why lower with AMF:** in squash and pumpkin, mycorrhizal colonization peaked at *moderate* P. Summer squash peaked at 54% colonization with 50 kg/ha P fertilizer (BASU trial). Pumpkin colonization peaked at **25% of the recommended P rate**. The plants also already have three P sources: struvite in the charged biochar, ~0.5–0.7 g P from the season's urine, and vermicast.

**How (AMF-friendly version):** use the bone-ash guide's **budget recipe**. For each 100 g of bone ash, use 6–8 g citric acid in 100–150 mL water. React ~24 h (P is only *partly* dissolved), then mix with about the same volume of vermicast (**1:1 to 1:2 ash:vermicast**; rock phosphate : compost at 50:50 performed best in Ditta et al. 2017) and **apply the same day**. Don't let it sit: dissolved P re-precipitates as pH rises (bone-ash guide §8, §14). **Mix it into a ring of soil** (~1–2 L of soil per plant) **15–25 cm from the stem** (the outer root zone, where hyphae forage), 5–10 cm deep. Don't bury it as a concentrated pocket: slowly dissolving calcium phosphates dissolve faster when spread through soil, because Ca and P build up around clumped particles and stall the reaction (Kanabo & Gilkes; FAO 2004), and alkaline ash in a pocket raises the local pH (bone-ash guide §15 placement). **Apply once**, ≥4 weeks after the plant was inoculated and before ~40 DAS (timing logic in §7). Give an optional half-dose at first fruit set only if leaves show P deficiency (dull, dark blue-green, small).

## 4. Potassium — The Real Gap

### The budget

| | g K₂O per plant |
|---|---|
| Crop uptake (9.3 g K × 1.2) | **~11** |
| UGA squash rec., medium K soil (90 lb/acre) | ~10 |
| Urine supplies (1.5 L × ~1.6 g K₂O/L, Rich Earth) | ~2.4 |
| Vermicast + soil (Río Sereno/Caisán rated *medium* K, Lorenzo 2013; Cerro Punta farms rated *high* K, 210–567 mg/L, Herrera et al. 2021) | unknown — could cover most of the gap |
| **Gap to cover** | **~6–8 g K₂O if soil K is low/medium; possibly ~0 if high** |

Why it matters: **urine-only pumpkins grew big vines but set less fruit**, which the authors attributed to low K or high chloride (Pradhan et al. 2009). Adding wood ash to urine fixed this in the same group's tomato trial (Pradhan et al. 2010).

**Soil test first.** The Cerro Punta lab data (IDIAP; farms heavily manured with chicken manure) rated K *high* at all five sites, so SOP fills a real gap only if your plot tests low or medium in K. If it tests high, urine + vermicast may be enough and SOP becomes optional. (A Cerro Punta preprint, Ohiaeri 2025, found soil K correlated with potato plant count, r = 0.70, but it used a home colorimetric kit on 10 plots per crop, so treat it as weak evidence.)

### Options compared

| Source | K₂O | Organic status (USDA NOP / EU / Canada) | pH effect | Fit for you |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Potassium sulfate (SOP, K₂SO₄)** | 50–52% (+17–18% S) | **Allowed if natural**: mined or brine-evaporated. Made with sulfuric acid (Mannheim process, common in Chinese SOP) = synthetic, **not** organic. | Neutral | **Best main source.** Chloride-free, no lime, no Mg. |
| Langbeinite (K-Mag / Sul-Po-Mag) | 22% (+18% MgO, 22% S) | Allowed (mined) | Neutral | **Skip.** It adds Mg on top of your Epsom, and Mg competes with K. |
| Potassium chloride (muriate, 0-0-60) | 60% | NOP: only if mined *and* "applied in a manner that minimizes chloride accumulation" | Neutral | **Avoid.** Urine already adds Cl, and Cl was the suspect in the pumpkin fruit-set drop. The Do it Center "0-0-60" is KCl. |
| **Wood ash** | 1–10% (typ. ~3–5%) + 17–28% Ca | Allowed (untreated wood only) | **Strong liming** (57–89% CaCO₃-equivalent; reacts fast) | Small top-up only (see below) |
| Coffee pulp / husk (Volcán is coffee country) | pulp ~3.6% K dry, husk ~2.5% K; >90% of the K is released, as available as KCl | Allowed | Mild | **Good local mulch/compost** in harvest season (Nov–Feb) |
| Comfrey / banana pseudostem mulch | K-rich, values vary | Allowed | Neutral | Slow, supplementary |

### Why wood ash can't be the main K source at pH 6.6

- Covering 7 g K₂O with ~5%-K₂O ash = **~140 g ash per plant/m²**. That is above the extension ceiling of **15–20 lb/1,000 ft² ≈ 75–100 g/m² per year** (UW-Madison), and equals roughly **1–1.2 t/ha of lime**.
- Alabama Extension: use ash as a K source **only on soils below pH 6.0**. UConn: **don't apply ash above pH 6.8**. Your soil is 6.6, your ceiling is 7.0 (see soil-ph.md), and bone ash and biochar also push pH up.
- Wood ash also varies 10-fold in K (1.3–9.1% K₂O across Alabama samples). Ash that has been rained on loses K.

### Should you burn biochar for ash? No.

- **Burning doesn't add K. It only destroys the carbon.** Wood biochar has little K to begin with: 0.16% K for softwood biochar without added ash (Grafmüller et al. 2022), so 1 kg of biochar holds ~1.6 g K. Burning it leaves the same ~1.6 g K in ~20–50 g of ash.
- That K is **already largely available** inside the biochar. At least 47% of biochar K is water-soluble (Kong 2014, in Bilias 2023 review), and 89–98% is citric-soluble in manure biochars (Andisol study, 2024).
- To cover a 7 g K₂O gap you would burn **3–5 kg of biochar per plant**, giving up the soil-carbon and CEC benefits that are the reason you made it.
- If you want ash, burn **prunings and firewood** (untreated, from clean sites). Never burn painted or treated wood, plastics or garbage, which concentrate heavy metals (UW-Madison).

### Recommended K program (per plant)

**Option A — SOP only (simplest, pH-neutral):** about **15 g SOP per plant per crop**, in three 5 g doses (weigh it once: a level teaspoon of granular SOP is ~6–8 g, so use a scant teaspoon). Squash responds to split K (UGA).

| When | SOP |
|---|---|
| First flowers (~45 DAS) | 5 g |
| ~2 weeks later (fruit set) | 5 g |
| After first harvest week (~70 DAS) | 5 g |

Scratch it into the root ring and water in. It can go on the **same day as urine** (sulfate doesn't react with ammonium). Don't let dry granules touch the stem.

**Option B — SOP + wood ash top-up:** 2 × 5 g SOP + **~30–40 g sifted hardwood ash per plant per season** (2–3 tablespoons, split 2–3 times). The ash adds ~1.5–2 g K₂O plus Ca. Check soil pH monthly and stop the ash if it reaches 6.9. Keep ash **≥3 days apart from urine** (its alkalinity drives ammonia loss).

**Option C — local organic:** mulch each plant with **~1 kg fresh coffee pulp** (≈200 g dry ≈ 7 g K) when it is available, plus one 5 g SOP dose at first flowers as insurance.

**Buying SOP in Panama:** soluble SOP is listed by Disagro Panamá. Ask for **"sulfato de potasio de origen natural/mineral (no Mannheim)"** if you want organic-grade; EU "SOP ecológico" products are natural-origin. Chemically K₂SO₄ is identical either way. The organic difference is the manufacturing process, which only matters if you certify.

## 5. Calcium, Magnesium and the Cation Balance

### How much Ca, and where it comes from

| Item | Per plant |
|---|---|
| Crop Ca uptake (Purquerio 2019) | **8.0 g**, peak ~0.33 g/day at ~41 days after transplant |
| Ca exported in fruit | ~4.3 g (43 kg/ha) |
| 15 g bone ash (~37% Ca) | ~5.5 g Ca. The citric-dissolved part is soluble Ca-citrate; the rest is slow hydroxyapatite. |
| Vermicast + soil exchangeable Ca | Usually the largest pool. Andisols in the Chiriquí highlands range from low-Ca (pH 5.9 plots, Lorenzo 2013) to well-supplied (Cerro Punta, high OM). |
| Wood ash (if used) | 17–28% Ca |

**Is bone ash enough?** Together with the soil and vermicast, yes, for a pH-6.6 Andisol with high organic matter. Bone ash alone (5.5 g) would not cover the 8 g. It does not need to: Ca is almost never short in a pH-6.6 soil. Nebraska Extension: *"calcium exists in abundance in our soils, so applying even more won't help."*

### Ca problems are about *delivery*, not supply

Ca moves only in the transpiration stream (xylem), so it goes where water goes:
- **High humidity cuts Ca to leaves.** In cucumber, leaf Ca deficiency tracked the 24-h vapour-pressure deficit (0.44–0.88 kPa). At low VPD, Ca must be **≥40% of root-zone cations** (vs 25% at high VPD), and EC should be kept ≤2.0 dS/m (Barker 1988). Volcán's 74–94% RH is a low-VPD environment. In tomato, high *day* humidity actually **raised** fruit Ca, because leaves took less (Ho/Adams). So in Volcán the risk is in **young leaves and growing tips**, not fruit.
- **Competing cations and salts cut Ca uptake.** K and Mg compete with Ca (Jakobsen 1993; Qu 2023), and **ammonium** competes too. In zucchini during flowering, Ca, Mg and K uptake fell as the NH₄⁺ share of N rose (J. Plant Nutr. 1999). Aged urine is ammonium. Weekly *small* diluted doses, plus nitrification in aerobic soil within days, keep this small. A big urine slug right before flowering is the thing to avoid.
- **Uneven watering** is the classic blossom-end-rot trigger in zucchini (SDSU, Cornell). Early fruit is often affected while later fruit is fine.
- **Excess N** also inhibits Ca uptake and promotes BER.

**Ca deficiency signs in zucchini:** cupped or crinkled young leaves with brown margins, dying growing tips, flower or young-fruit abortion, rot at the blossom end of fruit. **First fix:** even watering and a skipped urine week. Add Ca only if it persists.

**If Ca does turn out short:** use **gypsum** (CaSO₄·2H₂O, ~21–23% Ca, *yeso agrícola*, allowed in organic production). It is pH-neutral, adds no P (so it doesn't suppress mycorrhizae) and dissolves readily. Dose: **~10–20 g per plant** scratched into the root ring. Organic field trials show gypsum raises soil Ca and S within months but rarely raises yield unless Ca is truly low (Fleuridor et al. 2021; Ohio muck-soil trials). It can also push Mg off the exchange sites, which is another reason not to use it pre-emptively. Avoid eggshell or lime here: like wood ash, they raise pH, and you're at 6.6 with a 7.0 ceiling.

### Does Epsom in the urine hurt Ca? No, not at ≤5 g/L.

| | Mg added | Effect on Ca |
|---|---|---|
| Your Epsom (1.5 L urine × 5 g/L) | **0.74 g Mg/plant ≈ 7 kg Mg/ha per season**. Much of it is locked in slow struvite. | Negligible |
| Kasinath et al. 2014 (field tomato) | fruit Ca fell progressively as Mg rose toward **250 kg Mg/ha**; optimum 100 kg Mg + 250 kg Ca/ha | Your rate is ~3–7% of the rates studied |
| Qu et al. 2023 (hydroponic tomato and cucumber) | **2 mM Mg = optimum**; 3 mM cut K and Ca | A brief, diluted soil pulse buffered by CEC is not a standing 3 mM solution |

**Ratio check:** you add ~5.5 g Ca from bone ash vs 0.74 g Mg from Epsom, ≈7:1 by mass, so Ca stays dominant. The *bigger* competitor for Ca in this program is **K from SOP** (~6 g K). That is still balanced, but it's another reason to keep SOP at ~15 g and Epsom at ≤5 g/L. **Do not raise Epsom.**

**Keep K ≥ Mg in what you add.** Excess Mg (Epsom >5 g/L, langbeinite) suppresses K and Ca uptake. Excess K (heavy ash, or SOP well above ~20 g/plant) does the same to Mg and Ca. Mg toxicities are rare; the damage is always *induced deficiency* of the neighbour cation.

## 6. Compatibility Rules & Symptoms

| Combination | Rule |
|---|---|
| Urine + bone-ash slurry | **≥3 days apart.** Aged urine (pH 9) neutralizes the acid and re-precipitates the P; the acid dissolves the urine's struvite. |
| Urine + wood ash | **≥3 days apart.** Ash raises pH, so ammonia is lost. |
| Urine + SOP | Same day is fine |
| Bone-ash slurry + roots/AMF | Never raw (pH 3). Mix into vermicast first (apply the same day), and mix it into a ring of soil at the root-zone edge, not on the inoculated core. |
| Placement by product | **Slow bone ash + vermicast:** mixed into a soil ring (insoluble Ca-P dissolves better spread through soil). **Acidified fast-P dose:** narrow pocket near active roots (placement helps soluble P). **Charged biochar + vermicast:** pockets mixed with the hole's soil (~5–25% biochar by volume), 10–20 cm from the stem; root-zone hotspots of nutrient-loaded biochar beat soil-mixed or broadcast (Grafmüller 2022 cabbage +21%; Yeboah 2020 cowpea). Keep fresh urine-charged biochar off the stem (ammonium/salt zone, Sica 2025). Bone-ash bibliography refs 81–90. |
| Soluble P + young mycorrhizae | Not during the first ~4 weeks after inoculation (§7). |
| Great White + citric slurry, wood ash, or fresh urine | Not in the same spot or on the same day. Inoculum goes on moist soil near new roots. |
| Bone ash + wood ash *during* the acid reaction | Never (stops P release). After the reaction is fine. |

| Symptom | Likely cause | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Huge dark leaves, few female flowers | Too much N | Skip a urine week |
| Uniform pale older leaves | Low N | Keep the full urine dose |
| Yellow/scorched margins on older leaves, small or misshapen fruit | **Low K** | SOP dose now |
| Interveinal yellowing on older leaves | Low Mg | Check K isn't excessive first |
| Dull dark blue-green, small leaves, slow | Low P | Optional ½ bone-ash dose |
| Cupped/crinkled young leaves with brown edges, dying tips, aborted young fruit, blossom-end rot | Ca *delivery* (uneven water, humidity, NH₄⁺/K excess) | Even watering, skip a urine week; gypsum 10–20 g only if it persists |

## 7. Mycorrhizae (Great White): When and How to Get the Most From Them

### Why it matters more on your soil

- On P-fixing Andisols, mycorrhizal hyphae reach P that roots can't. Inoculated salak seedlings on an Andisol had **53% colonization with organic fertilizer but only 20% with inorganic NPK**, and twice the P uptake. On a Chilean Andisol, soluble P cut the inoculum's P-uptake benefit, while partially acidulated rock phosphate worked best with the fungus (Rubio et al. 2003).
- **With AMF, bone char = superphosphate.** In a P-fixing soil, AM-inoculated maize took up as much P from bone char as from TSP. Without AMF, TSP won (Zwetsloot et al. 2016, Cornell). **This is the key reason not to fully dissolve your bone ash:** the fungi plus root exudates do the dissolving, in step with the plant's demand.
- **Zucchini responds, but it depends on fungus × hybrid.** In 4 zucchini hybrids, *Gigaspora margarita* gave the best mycorrhizal dependency (up to +30%), while *Glomus intraradices* was *negative* in some hybrids (J. Anim. Plant Sci. 2011). Great White contains both, plus 7 other endo species, and mixtures hedge this. *Funneliformis mosseae* raised *C. pepo* growth and fruit yield in pots (Diversity 2022).

### Great White composition and dose

- Endo species (the ones that matter for zucchini): *Glomus aggregatum, intraradices, mosseae, etunicatum* at 83 propagules/g each, plus *G. clarum, monosporum, deserticola, Paraglomus brasilianum, Gigaspora margarita* at 11/g each: **≈387 endo propagules/g** (see three-sisters-volcan.md). The ecto species are for trees and are irrelevant here. It also contains *Bacillus*, *Paenibacillus*, *Pseudomonas*, *Azotobacter* and *Trichoderma*.
- Label: dust the planting hole; as a drench, **1 tsp (3.3–5 g) per 2 gal (~8–9 L) of water**. One teaspoon ≈ 1,300–1,900 endo propagules.
- **Per established plant (top-up):** ~½ tsp (~2 g ≈ 750 propagules) per plant. Either side-band it 10–15 cm from the stem, 5–8 cm deep, in contact with live roots, or drench ~1 L per plant of the label-rate suspension. In cotton, a side band applied 2 weeks after planting still raised growth (Afek et al. 1990).
- **Trichoderma caveat:** in melon (a cucurbit), *T. harzianum* *reduced* *G. intraradices* colonization. Elsewhere it was neutral or synergistic. You can't separate them in Great White, so just don't add a second Trichoderma product on top.

### Timeline: what happens when

| Days after inoculation | What's happening | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 1–4 (established inoculum) | Hyphal contact → root penetration → first arbuscules | Brundrett et al. 1985 (leek) |
| 3–12 (from spores/propagules) | Colonization starts; *G. deserticola* ~3 d, *G. mosseae/intraradices* 6–12 d | Afek et al. 1990 |
| 0–~25 | **Lag phase.** Low colonization; the fungus costs carbon (tomato shoots −25–30% at 19 days, recovered by 35 days) | Sutton 1973; J. Plant Growth Regul. 2026 |
| ~25–60 | **Rapid phase.** Colonization climbs to 48–84% (field crops); full establishment ~27 days in tomato; native AMF peak at 45–60 days in vegetables | Sutton 1973; 2026 tomato; Edathil (Pertanika) |
| 60+ | Plateau | — |

**Earlier is better:** pepper inoculated *at seeding* yielded **+17% more fruit** in the field than pepper inoculated at transplant, because P uptake started weeks earlier (Waterer & Coltman 1989). Inoculum placed **~3 cm below the seed** colonized best (Afek 1990). **For future zucchini:** coat or dust the seed or plug at sowing, and put a pinch 3 cm under it.

### What to give early: "just enough" for plant *and* fungus

| Input | Effect on AMF | Do |
|---|---|---|
| **Soluble P** (fully dissolved citric bone ash, superphosphate) | The strongest suppressor. High P makes the *root* refuse the fungus, acting systemically through shoot P with a >1-week lag (Balzergue 2013; Breuillin 2010). P applied **1 week after planting** suppressed hyphae and colonization **more** than the same P applied before planting (Oxisol study, Plant Soil). | **No soluble P pulses during weeks 0–4 after inoculation.** Afterwards, only slow or partial P, placed at the root-zone edge. |
| **A small starter P** | In very P-poor (tropical) soils, *too little* P also blocks colonization; a little available P "primes" the symbiosis (Ahiabor & Hirata; Bolan 1984) | **Already covered** by the struvite-charged biochar + vermicast at planting. Ideal. |
| Soil P level | Colonization inhibited above **~11–31 mg/kg Olsen-P** in maize/wheat (Deng et al. 2017). Root and AMF P-foraging switches on below ~10 mg/kg (maize, PLOS One 2014). | Aim for "adequate, not high". Andisol fixation keeps solution P low anyway. A soil test would tell you where you sit. |
| **N (urine)** | N addition cut root colonization **−11.6%** on average (meta-analysis, 94 studies) | Small weekly doses, not big pulses. N is not the enemy at your rates. |
| **Biochar** | Raises colonization overall (meta-analysis, 85 studies), especially low-EC, low-nutrient biochar. Biochar + AMF raised maize P uptake **67% more than the two effects summed** (no-P soil). *Paraglomus* (in Great White) colonizes biochar pores. | Keep using it. Note that *wood* biochar mixed with bone char cut resin-P by 14–26% (Zwetsloot 2016), so **keep bone ash (soil ring) and biochar (pockets) in separate spots**, not pre-mixed. |
| **Vermicast / compost** | Raises colonization overall, **but suppressed it in neutral-pH soils (6.5–7.5)** in the same meta-analysis. Your soil is 6.6. | Moderate amounts. It's your Ca/K/micronutrient and P-protection carrier, not a reason to pile it on. |
| Citric acid | Low concentrations (~10 mM) + AMF gave the best nutrient extraction (bone-ash guide §14). A pH-3 slurry on roots kills colonization. | Only the vermicast-buffered slurry, at the root-zone edge |
| Wood ash / pH | AMF are fine at pH 6–7. Large pH jumps disturb them. | Keep pH <7; ash only as a small K top-up |
| Tillage, bare soil, drying out | Breaks the hyphal network | Mulch, no digging near plants, keep soil evenly moist |

### Does vermicast + charged biochar added at ~3 weeks still help the AMF? Yes, mildly, and the timing is fine.

- **Struvite is the AMF-friendly form of P.** Its water solubility is <3%, and it dissolves ~4× slower than MAP. At a high P rate, struvite **inhibited AMF colonization less than MAP** (alfalfa, Thiessen Martens, U. Manitoba). Mycorrhizal tomato grew **24% more shoot on struvite than on MAP**, with 26% more P uptake (Di Tomassi et al. 2021). Caveat: in rye, AMF did not increase P uptake from struvite (Schwalb et al. 2021). So it's a *gentle* P source, not a guaranteed AMF booster.
- **Arriving at ~19 DAS = the start of the rapid colonization phase** (~25–60 days). A slow P source there doesn't produce the soluble-P pulse that blocks new entry points (that risk is concentrated in the first 1–2 weeks after inoculation, which had already passed).
- **Biochar** raises colonization on average (meta-analysis), and *Paraglomus* (in Great White) colonizes its pores. But the meta-analysis found the benefit is largest for **low-EC, low-nutrient** biochar, and urine-charged biochar is the opposite at first (salts, NH₄⁺). Expect its AMF benefit to grow as Volcán's rain leaches the salts.
- **Vermicast** raises colonization overall. In neutral soils (pH 6.5–7.5) compost/manure sometimes suppressed it, so it's helpful in moderate amounts, not more.
- **Net effect:** it neither hurt nor "armed" the fungi much right away. It gave a slow P/N base the fungi can work with. It's also a reason to keep the bone ash **light (~15 g)** and **away from the biochar pockets**.

### Recommended AMF-first sequence

1. **At sowing:** Great White on or under the seed, plus starter P (struvite-charged biochar + vermicast). *(2026 plants: Great White at sowing ✔. The vermicast + charged biochar went in later, at ~3 weeks; see below.)*
2. **Weeks 0–4:** N only (small weekly urine). **No bone ash, no soluble P.**
3. **Weeks 4–5 (≥4 weeks after inoculation, before ~40 DAS):** one **lightly acidified** bone-ash dose (~15 g/plant, 6–8% citric, ~24 h — a citrate primer that dissolves <5% of the P, not a solubilizer; see bone-ash guide §8; mixed ~1:1 with vermicast and applied the same day) mixed into a soil ring 15–25 cm out, where hyphae forage. AMF actively proliferate in P-enriched patches (Cui & Caldwell 1996). If the plants were **not** inoculated at sowing, use **raw** finely ground bone ash at the same spot and inoculate now.
4. **From first flowers:** K (SOP) and the usual urine. Optional half bone-ash dose only on P-deficiency signs.
5. **Next crop:** don't till. Plant into the same mycorrhizal network; residual bone ash is now fungal food.
