# Cilantro (Coriander) Transplant Spacing & Pine-Needle Mulch — Literature Review

> Compiled from university extension guidance and published field research worldwide.
> Focus: organic, cool-highland growing at Volcán, Panama (~1,400 m elevation — cool air, intense sun).
> Companion to `cilantro-coriander-fertilizer.md` (which covers transplant nutrition).

---

## 1. Executive Summary

Two questions, three answers:

1. **Transplant spacing: 6–8 in (15–20 cm) apart**, ~12 in between rows. Wider (8–12 in) only if growing for coriander seed.
2. **Pine needles: yes — an excellent mulch for cilantro.** The "pine needles acidify soil" belief is a debunked myth; and pine needles keep soil *cool*, which directly delays cilantro's bolt trigger.
3. **The leaf-burn you observed is real and documented.** It is caused by radiant/reflected heat from glossy or light-colored mulch surfaces (and by wet mulch against leaves), not by "acidity." It is prevented with a bare ring around stems, aged needles, a thin layer around herbs, and afternoon shade.

Caveat that shapes everything: **cilantro transplants poorly** (long taproot; root disturbance accelerates bolting). Direct seeding is preferred; if transplanting, move the whole rootball intact and keep the crown at the soil line.

## 2. Spacing (Transplants & Direct Seed)

| Source | Leaf spacing | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Clemson HGIC | 6–8 in | 1 ft row spacing |
| Oregon State Ext. | thin to 8 in | rows 18–24 in |
| Wisconsin Hort. | 3–6 in | 12 in for seed production |
| Utah State Ext. | 2 in (dense) | 8–10 in for coriander seed; rows ≥15 in |
| Gardeners Path | 3–12 in, "commonly 6–8 in" | 12 in for seed; 1 plant per square-foot center |
| MAWEB (transplant guide) | 6–8 in leaf / 8–12 in seed | transplant at 2–4 true leaves |

**Consensus for leaf harvest: 6–8 in (15–20 cm), ~12 in rows.**

Two nuances behind that number:

1. **Transplant shock ≈ bolting.** Oregon State, Wisconsin, and ZonePlant all warn cilantro "does not transplant well because of its long taproot" and that even mild root disturbance accelerates bolting. Transplants need an intact, generous rootball; the crown is left at soil line (never buried deep).
2. **Dense planting shades roots and delays bolting — but costs airflow.** Gardening Know How notes cilantro can be grown as tight as **3–4 in** because "the leaves will shade the roots and help keep the plants from bolting in hot weather." Wider spacing (6–8 in) maximizes airflow, which reduces fungal/bacterial leaf-spot pressure.

**Volcán recommendation:** in the cool highlands, heat-bolting is less of a threat than trapped-humidity leaf disease. Transplant at **6–8 in apart**, ~12 in between rows; if direct-sowing, 4–6 in is safe.

## 3. Pine-Needle Mulch — Myth vs. Reality

### 3.1 The acidity myth is debunked

Every major extension service that has tested it reaches the same conclusion: pine-needle mulch does **not** significantly acidify garden soil. Documented pH shift is **< 0.3 units after 2–5 years of continuous application** — often no measurable change at all.

| Finding | Source |
|---|---|
| Needles pH 3.2–3.8 when green/fresh; acidic compounds neutralized by soil microbes during decomposition; brown fallen needles near-neutral | Oregon State Ext.; NC State (Lee Co.) |
| pH shift < 0.3 units after 2–5 yrs; many trials show no change | WSU (Chalker-Scott, *Horticultural Myths*); U. New Hampshire; Colorado State; Master Gardeners of Northern Virginia |
| Pine *forests* are acidic because of parent rock, centuries of accumulated humus, and self-reinforcing acid-tolerant ecology — not because of fresh needles on the surface | GrowPerma review (2026); U. Maryland (Ristvey) |
| "Any acids in the pine needles will be broken down during decomposition" | OSU Extension (Erica Chernoh) |

**Practical note:** the only scenario where fresh *green* needles matter is if you till a large volume into the soil immediately (a brief, non-damaging pH dip). As surface mulch, brown needles are effectively neutral.

### 3.2 Why pine needles are *ideal* for cilantro specifically

Cilantro bolts when **soil temperature reaches 75°F (24°C)** — a soil-temperature trigger, not an air-temperature one. Pine straw keeps soil measurably cooler and moister, directly attacking the bolt trigger. Gardening Know How explicitly lists **pine straw** among recommended cilantro mulches, alongside straw and shredded leaves.

- One source measured bare full-sun soil ~78°F vs. ~66–68°F under 3 in of straw mulch — the difference between leafy growth and a flower stalk.
- Pine needles are light, don't mat into an impermeable crust, allow water + air through, and decompose slowly (12–18 months), so they don't tie up nitrogen the way fresh wood chips can.

### 3.3 Drawbacks to respect

| Concern | Reality |
|---|---|
| Poor soil insulator (thin needles) | True — less winter insulation than straw/leaves; minor concern in Volcán's frost-free climate |
| Slow nutrient contribution | True — adds organic matter only over years |
| Flammable when dry | Real — keep away from structures; keep damp in dry periods |
| Can harbor insects (cockroaches, earwigs, centipedes) | Real — keep layer ≤3 in and away from the house |
| **Reflective/radiant surface heat** | Real — see §4; this is your leaf-burn observation |

## 4. The Leaf-Burn Observation (Mulch Contact in Full Sun)

Your observation is corroborated by the literature, with two distinct mechanisms:

### 4.1 Radiant heat from the mulch surface

The classic reference is a USDA Forest Service study of mulched spruce seedlings. On a warm July day when ambient air was 81°F:

- **125°F within 1 cm of a flat, glossy spruce-needle litter surface** (vs. 108°F over bare soil, 105°F over bare dry soil).
- Light-colored or reflective mulch surfaces raise *air temperature above the mulch* and can cause heat injury to tender foliage lying against them; the damage progresses from lower stem/branches upward, roots remaining healthy.

This is why **glossy, flat, fresh needles** are flagged as worse than other mulches — the reflective surface radiates heat onto low foliage. Thin-leaved, heat-sensitive plants (cilantro, seedlings) are exactly the vulnerable category.

### 4.2 Moisture-mediated scalding / rot

Wet mulch in direct contact with leaf tissue, under full sun, creates a hot, humid micro-environment that causes leaf scorch and rot — especially with tender herbs. UF/IFAS's guidance is directly on point: **"When mulching seedlings or small vegetables or herbs, apply mulch at a depth of 1 inch or less,"** and never let mulch touch the stem or foliage.

### 4.3 Why Volcán amplifies this

At ~1,400 m elevation, UV and radiant intensity are high even while air stays cool — so the "cool soil" benefit of pine needles and the "hot reflective surface" risk can occur on the same afternoon. Cool air ≠ cool mulch surface in direct tropical-highland sun.

### 4.4 How to get the benefits without the burn

1. **Keep a 2–3 in bare ring** around each plant's stem/crown; never let foliage rest directly on the needles.
2. **Use aged (brown, matte) needles**, not fresh green glossy ones — they reflect less.
3. **Apply thin (~1 in) around young herbs/seedlings**; thicker (2–3 in) only once plants are established and lifted off the surface.
4. **Add afternoon shade** (30% shade cloth, or interplant near taller crops) in the hottest months — standard bolt-prevention and it removes the direct mid-afternoon radiant load on the mulch surface.
5. **Water at the base, not overhead** — wet foliage is the main driver of cilantro bacterial leaf spot, and it worsens the wet-mulch scald effect.

## 5. Practical Recommendations (Volcán)

| Decision | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| Spacing (transplant) | 6–8 in apart, ~12 in rows |
| Spacing (direct seed) | 4–6 in, thinned as needed |
| Seed production | 8–12 in apart |
| Mulch | Aged pine needles, 2–3 in (thin to ~1 in around young herbs) |
| Stem clearance | 2–3 in bare ring, no "volcano mulching" |
| Shade | Morning/late sun; 30% shade or taller-neighbor afternoon shade in hot spells |
| Watering | Base/drip irrigation; ~1 in/week; avoid overhead |
| Transplant method | Whole intact rootball; crown at soil line; direct-seed future successions |
