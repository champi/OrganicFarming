# Banana Planting in Volcán: Sucker Choice, Size, Spacing and Hole

Practical planting guide for bananas and plantains (*Musa* spp.) from suckers ("hijos", "colinos") on Volcán's Andisols (~1,400 m, cool, wet, deep, well-drained volcanic soil).

---

## 1. Quick answers

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Right name? | Yes: **sword sucker** (Spanish: *hijo de espada*, *colino aguja/puyón*). The one to avoid is the **water sucker** (*hijo de agua*, *bandera*). Tiny shoots with only scale leaves are **peepers** (*cebollín*/"botón"). |
| Ideal "seed" size | Sword sucker **0.5–1.5 m tall**, corm **1–2 kg** after trimming, corm girth **≥ 30 cm**. Lower limit ~0.5 kg in a 30 cm hole. Don't plant anything under 30 cm tall. |
| Spacing | **3 × 3 m** for tall types (Gros Michel, plantain, Manzano). **2.5 × 2.5 m** for dwarf Cavendish types. On slopes: staggered triangle along the contour. |
| 40 × 40 × 40 cm hole? | **Yes, that's a good size** for a 1–1.5 kg sucker in Volcán's loose Andisol. Use 30 × 30 × 30 cm for small suckers (0.5–1 kg). Digging deeper (60 cm) gave no benefit for suckers in a field trial. |

---

## 2. How to tell a sword sucker from a water sucker

Suckers come from buds on the underground stem (corm or rhizome). While the mother plant is alive and growing, apical dominance holds back its buds, so the suckers that grow out stay narrow-leaved for a long time and build a big corm first. Those are sword suckers. Water suckers come from buds on old, decaying corms, usually after the stem above was harvested or died. Nothing holds them back, so they put out broad adult leaves right away from a small corm (ProMusa; ITFnet; UPR manual).

| Feature | Sword sucker (choose) | Water sucker (avoid) |
|---|---|---|
| Leaves | Narrow, lance- or sword-shaped, with a midrib and a very thin blade, even at 0.5–1 m tall | Broad, adult-shaped leaves while the plant is still short |
| Pseudostem | Cone-shaped: wide, hard base that tapers to a point | Thin, about the same width top to bottom, soft |
| Corm | Large, conical or round, heavy for its height | Small and light, few roots |
| Attachment | Deep, firm connection to a **living, vigorous** mother plant | Shallow, weak connection, often to an old harvested stump |
| Where it appears | Close to the mother, growing upright | Often from an old cut stump, sometimes leaning |

**Field test:** compare leaf width with height. A shoot about 1 m tall that still has narrow, strap-like leaves is a sword sucker. A shoot of the same height with wide, paddle-like leaves is a water sucker. Then dig: a sword sucker has a corm much wider than its pseudostem.

**Nuance:** ProMusa cites Oppenheimer & Gottreich: sword and water suckers of the same height, once cut from the mother, gave similar bunches. Rojas & Vargas (2005, Costa Rica) found the same in plantain. The main reason to avoid water suckers is that they are small and weakly anchored, which makes them bad **followers** (*hijos de reemplazo*) in an existing clump. As planting material, a big water sucker is acceptable. Choose the sword sucker when you can.

## 3. Ideal size (weight and height)

| Source | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| FIEDS / INIAP plantain guide (Ecuador) | Sword sucker **0.5–1.0 m** tall, **1–2 kg** |
| Embrapa (Brazil) | 0.5–1.0 kg suckers → 30 × 30 × 30 cm hole; 1.0–1.5 kg → 40 × 40 × 40 cm |
| NRCB (India) | Sword suckers 3–5 months old, **1–1.5 kg**; 1.5–2 kg for tall long-cycle cultivars |
| TNAU (India) | 3–4-month-old sword sucker, 1.5–2 kg |
| UNA Nicaragua (plantain) | Corm 1–2 kg; larger isn't worth it |
| UF/IFAS (Florida) | Sword suckers 1.2–1.5 m tall; large sword suckers and maidenheads preferred |
| Stover & Simmonds via ITFnet | Minimum **15 cm** corm diameter and **50 cm** height; peepers < 30 cm are slow, may die, and give small first bunches |
| Berrill 1960 (Cavendish trial) | Sword suckers with a corm girth of **12 in (30 cm)** were the best choice. Each +5 cm of girth brought flowering about 12 days earlier, but the largest suckers gave slightly **smaller** bunches |
| O'Farrell et al. 1989 (Williams) | Suckers of 0.25–0.5, 0.5–1.0 and 1.0–1.5 kg gave the **same average yield over two cycles**. Larger suckers yielded earlier, then lost ground in the first ratoon |

**What the research shows:** above a minimum size, sucker size mostly changes **how fast** the plant crops, not **how much**. Too small (peepers, < 0.5 kg) means slow establishment and losses. Too large (> 2 kg, 2 m) is heavy to carry, harder to clean, and the first bunch is not bigger.

**Grade before planting:** sort suckers into small, medium and large, and plant each size together. Big suckers otherwise shade the small ones, and harvests spread out (DESCO; UPR; UNA). For family use, a mix of sizes is actually useful because it staggers the harvest (Staver/CIRAD).

**Volcán note:** in cool highlands, planting to flowering stretches from about 7–9 months to **up to 18 months** (FAO). Bunch filling also takes longer: 140–160 days above ~900 m vs. 107–130 days lower down (Turrialba, Costa Rica study). This argues for the upper end of the size range (**1–2 kg, about 1 m tall**) to win back a few weeks.

## 4. Preparing the sucker

1. **Dig out** the sucker with a sharp spade, cutting close to the mother's corm so you get as much of the sucker's corm as possible.
2. **Pare (peel) the corm:** cut off all roots and shave the surface until only **white** tissue is left. Cut away any brown, black or red spots and tunnels (banana weevil larvae, nematodes). If damage goes deep, throw the sucker away. Paring alone removed about 95% of weevil eggs, and no nematodes were found on pared suckers (Tinzaara et al., Uganda).
3. **Optional disinfestation** (organic-compatible):
   - Boiling water dip of **20–30 s** for pared suckers (Coyne et al. 2010, IITA). This is the easiest method.
   - Or hot water at **53–55 °C for 20 min** (NARO), which needs a thermometer.
4. **Cut the pseudostem** to about 10–20 cm above the corm and remove the leaves. This reduces water loss and wind rock.
5. Let the cut surfaces **dry in shade for 1–2 days**, away from the source field, so they don't pick up soft rot (ITFnet; UPR).
6. Take suckers only from **healthy, productive mother plants**. Fusarium wilt is spread by planting material. Gros Michel and Manzano/Silk are susceptible to Race 1 (Panama disease). Panama is actively surveilling for **TR4**: MIDA ran a Darién outbreak drill in Oct 2025 and early-detection training in Aug 2026. Don't bring suckers in from outside the area.

## 5. Spacing

| Source / context | Spacing | Plants/ha |
|---|---|---|
| IITA (general) | 3 × 3 m most varieties; dwarf 2 × 2 m; very large 4 × 4 m | 1,111 / 2,500 / 625 |
| NARO Uganda | 3 × 3 m or wider | 1,111 |
| Philippines BPI | Cavendish types / Latundan 3 × 3 m; Lakatan 2 × 2.5 m | 1,111 / 2,000 |
| Ecuador plantain (FIEDS) | 3 × 3, 2.5 × 2.5 or 3 × 2 m; ≤ 2,000/ha | 1,111–1,666 |
| UPR (mountain plantain) | 6 × 6 or 7 × 6 ft (1.8–2.1 m) | ~2,500 |
| UF/IFAS (home garden) | Dwarf ≥ 8 ft (2.4 m); large ≥ 12 ft (3.7 m) | — |

**Density trials:** closer spacing gives **more total kg per hectare** but **smaller bunches**:
- Nankinga et al. 2005 (Uganda): 2 × 2 m had the highest yield per area; 3 × 3 m had the heaviest bunches.
- Ndabamenye et al. 2013 (Rwanda highlands, Andosols included): yield kept rising with density in fertile, high-rainfall sites. Bunches were 18–21 kg at low density vs. 15 kg at high density. Higher density also drains nutrients faster, especially K.
- UF/IFAS notes that roots spread several metres. IITA says even dwarf types reach 6 m.

**Recommendation for Volcán:**
- **3 × 3 m** for Gros Michel, plantain and other tall types. Bigger bunches are easier to sell and handle. The wider spacing leaves room to intercrop in the first year (beans, squash) and lets light into a cool, cloudy site. Highland bananas also sucker more (Kamira 2016), so a mat fills its space.
- **2.5 × 2.5 m** for dwarf Cavendish types (Gran Enano, Williams, dwarf Dominico) if space is tight.
- On slopes, plant in a **staggered triangle (tres bolillos) along the contour**. Plant in blocks, not single long lines, so the plants shelter each other from wind (IITA).
- Keep **one mother + one follower + one peeper** per mat (UF/IFAS) to hold that density.

## 6. Planting hole: is 40 × 40 × 40 cm right?

**Yes.** Here is what the sources say:

| Source | Hole size |
|---|---|
| Embrapa (Brazil) | 30³ cm for 0.5–1.0 kg suckers; **40³ cm for 1.0–1.5 kg**; up to 60 cm deep only in heavy or compacted soil |
| FIEDS / INIAP (Ecuador plantain) | **40 × 40 × 40 cm** |
| DESCO (Peru) | 30³ cm in loose soil; **40³ cm in semi-compact soil** |
| NARO Uganda | Minimum 30³, commonly 45³, up to 60³ cm |
| Swennen 1990 (IITA) | Minimum 30 × 30 × 30 cm for pared suckers or in-vitro plants |
| UPR (Puerto Rico plantain) | No more than about 1 ft (30 cm). **Deep holes delay emergence and raise losses in rainy periods** |
| UF/IFAS (Florida sand) | 90 cm wide × 60 cm deep (poor sandy soil) |

**Key trial:** Sebuwufu, Rubaihayo & Blomme (2005, *InfoMusa*) compared 60 cm and 40 cm deep holes (60 cm wide) for 2 kg pared sword suckers. They found **no significant difference** in growth at 24 weeks or at flowering. Roots were distributed the same way. The 40 cm hole took about **50% less labour**. Banana roots grow mostly in the topsoil, so the important thing is to put the corm in rich topsoil, not to dig deep.

**For Volcán's Andisol** (deep, loose, well-drained), 40 × 40 × 40 cm is plenty for 1–2 kg suckers, and 30³ cm works for smaller ones. Only go bigger where you hit compacted subsoil (old tractor pan, road fill).

**How to plant:**
1. Keep the **topsoil** (first 15–20 cm) on one side and the **subsoil** on the other (DESCO; FIEDS).
2. Mix the topsoil with compost or worm castings, plus the P source (bone ash; see [garden-crops-bone-ash-sop-doses.md](garden-crops-bone-ash-sop-doses.md) for how P behaves in Andisols).
3. Put the topsoil mix in first, set the corm upright so its top is **about 5–10 cm below the surface**, and fill with the rest.
4. **Pack the soil firmly** so there are no air pockets, which fill with water and rot the corm (DESCO; UNA).
5. **On a slope:** turn the sucker so the cut scar (where it was attached to the mother) faces **downhill**. Followers then come out **uphill**, which slows the mat from climbing out of the soil ("high mat") (NARO; DESCO).
6. Mulch heavily (chop-and-drop), keep it a hand's width from the pseudostem, and plant at the **start of the rainy season**. Avoid the last months of the rains (NARO).

**Pest note:** banana weevil is worst between about 1,200 and 1,900 m and fades above that (NARO). Volcán is inside that band, so paring the corms is worth doing.
