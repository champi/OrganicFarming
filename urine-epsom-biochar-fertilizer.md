# Urine + Epsom Salt + Biochar: Charging, Magnesium, and Phosphorus Recovery on an Andisol — Worldwide Literature Review

> Compiled from peer-reviewed soil-science, sanitation, and agronomy literature worldwide.
> Focus: organic, living-soil approach for Volcán, Panama (Andisol, pH ~6.6, high P-fixation).
> Inputs available: human/cow urine, Epsom salt (MgSO₄·7H₂O), biochar, bone ash, vermicast, wood ash.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

Two things were researched and one common assumption is corrected:

1. **Urine-charged biochar is one of the best-documented low-tech fertilizers in the literature.** Urine is the nutrient *donor*; biochar is the *slow-release sponge*. The canonical results are a **+306% pumpkin yield** (Schmidt et al. 2015, urine:biochar 1:1 v/v) and **+60% cabbage/kohlrabi** (BUNCH 2021).

2. **Epsom salt in the mix does two *completely different* jobs that must not be conflated:**
   - **Magnesium nutrition** — correcting an Mg deficiency. Dose is set by a *soil test*, not by a fixed g/L.
   - **Phosphorus recovery** via struvite (Mg²⁺ + urine PO₄³⁻ + NH₄⁺ → MgNH₄PO₄·6H₂O). Dose is set by *Mg:P stoichiometry*.

3. **On a Volcán Andisol, the high-value use is P recovery, not Mg nutrition.** The soil fixes up to **83% of applied P** (Morán et al. 2022), and struvite is documented to *bypass that fixation* — it releases P slowly in "tropical soils with high phosphorus fixation" (Springer 2026). That is the single best reason to add Epsom salt to urine-biochar in Volcán at all.

4. **The commonly cited "3 g/L" is neither job's optimum.** It is ~296 mg/L Mg (Mg:P ≈ 0.8:1): too much to justify for Mg when the soil is already Mg-adequate (likely at pH 6.6), and too little to fully capture P as struvite (needs ≥1.2:1). It is a *harmless middle ground*, not an optimum.

5. **Fresh vs. stored urine changes everything.** Struvite forms only at **pH ~8.5–9.5** — i.e., stored/hydrolyzed urine (which self-raises to pH ~9) or in the presence of alkaline biochar. Epsom added to *fresh* urine (pH ~6) stays mostly in solution and only adds Mg + sulfate.

## 2. Urine-Charged Biochar: The Core Evidence

| Finding | Source |
|---|---|
| Urine:biochar 1:1 (v/v), low-dose root-zone (0.75 t/ha biochar) → **+306%** pumpkin yield vs. urine-only; +85% vs. biochar-only; exceeded conventional fertilized yields | Schmidt et al., MDPI *Agriculture* 5:723 (2015) |
| Urine:biochar 1:1 (v/v), applied 1.15–3 L/m² → **+60%** cabbage/kohlrabi yield | BUNCH, *Environ. Dev. Sustain.* Springer (2021) |

| Cattle-urine-enriched biochar + manure → **+62%** maize yield, N uptake **+125%** | MDPI *Agronomy/Soil Systems* (Nepal, 2024) |

| Urine forms an organic coating on biochar pore surfaces → improved nutrient capture/exchange | Schmidt et al. (2015) |
| Faecal-derived biochar enriched in N and P by adsorption/precipitation from human urine | Koulouri et al., *J. Environ. Manage.* (2024) |
| Rice-husk biochar adsorbed ~50% total N, ~70% P, ~80% K from urine; NH₄⁺ equilibrium ~24 h | Van Nguyen (2025); Kizito et al. (2015) |
| Lacto-fermented faeces + urine-charged biochar improved corn yield, bulk density, K, and nitrate | Wageningen PhD (2017) |

**Reading:** the correct model is *urine = nitrogen/fertility donor; biochar = carbon sponge + slow-release carrier*. Charging at **1:1 (v/v)** and applying at **≤10% of hole/mix volume** is the well-supported default. The biochar in the Schmidt trial was itself mineral-rich (Mg 12,000 mg/kg, Ca 17,000, K 28,000) — alkaline (pH 9.8), which matters for §4.2 below.

## 3. The Volcán Andisol Context

| Finding | Source |
|---|---|
| Chiriquí volcanic soils: **P retention up to 83.18%**, amorphous Fe/Al ≥2%, bulk density <0.9 g/cm³ → confirmed Andisols | Morán et al., *IESTEC* (2022) |
| P immobilization is "a significant fertility limitation of Andisols in Central America"; P retention >85% (NZ method), 96% (isotherm) | Molina et al., *Commun. Soil Sci. Plant Anal.* (1991) |
| Volcán/Barú soils: "volcanic origin, slightly acidic, good nutrient content, high OM"; rainfall 2,418–3,613 mm/yr; Volcán 1,300–1,500 m, 16–27 °C | MIDA (2021) |
| Chiriquí highland volcanic soils (Caisán, San Andrés, Río Sereno): pH 5.9, **low P, low Ca, and low Mg**, medium K | Lorenzo et al., *Agron. Mesoam.* (2013) |
| Cerro Punta soils: pH 6.0–6.9, high OM 20–60%; no significant NPK→growth correlation (fertility non-limiting, "ceiling effect") | Ohiaeri (2025) |

**Reading:** two facts dominate. **(a)** P fixation is the number-one fertility problem — any strategy that protects P from allophane/Al/Fe lock-up (struvite, biochar coating, mycorrhizae) pays disproportionately. **(b)** Mg status is *variable* across the highlands: some plots are documented low-Mg (pH 5.9), but the region's typical pH 6.0–6.9 with 20–60% OM suggests base saturation is usually adequate. **Your pH 6.6 is a good sign for Mg** — the only way to settle it is a Ca/Mg/K soil test.

## 4. Epsom Salt: Two Jobs, Two Different Doses

Epsom salt = MgSO₄·7H₂O: **9.86% Mg, 13% S**. Human urine already carries ~**50–100 mg/L Mg** fresh, dropping to <50 mg/L once stored (Mg precipitates out with Ca/P). Urine P ≈ **470–1,070 mg/L** (typically ~500 mg/L).

### 4.1 Job 1 — Magnesium Nutrition

| Finding | Source |
|---|---|
| Mg fertilization across 570 paired observations: yield **+8.5%** avg, but **+9.4%** only when exchangeable Mg <60 mg/kg and **+11.3%** when pH <6.5; only **+3.9–4.9%** when Mg >120 mg/kg (near noise) | Wang et al., *Frontiers Plant Sci.* (2020) meta-analysis |
| Mg deficiency likely when: pH <5.4, Mg <6% of CEC, K:Mg ratio >4, or exchangeable Mg <0.5 cmolc/kg | Ferreira et al., *Int. J. Plant Soil Sci.* (2023) |
| Apply Mg when soil test <35 ppm (sandy) / <50 ppm (fine) / <100 ppm (organic), or Mg <3% of exchangeable bases, or K% > Mg% | MSU Extension E-2934 |
| Critical soil Mg ≈ **74 ppm** for tomato (response below this) | Kasinath et al., *J. Hortl. Sci.* (2014) |
| MgSO₄ is the most effective Mg form (vs. other Mg salts); boosts chlorophyll and gas exchange | Borowski & Michałek, *Acta Agrobotanica* (2010) |
| **Excess Mg suppresses K and Ca uptake** (antagonism) | Guan et al., MDPI *Agronomy* 10:1863 (2020) |
| Plants grow normally across a *broad* Ca:Mg range (0.5–7.7); Mg rarely limits unless extreme | Zhang, Univ. Hawai'i (1999) |

**Reading:** Mg *only* pays when the soil is actually deficient. Thresholds to act on:

| Exchangeable Mg | Status | Action |
|---|---|---|
| **<60 ppm** (<0.5 cmolc/kg) | Deficient | Mg will pay — supplement |
| **60–120 ppm** | Moderate | marginal |
| **>120 ppm** | Sufficient | **skip Epsom** — over-Mg antagonizes your K (vermicast) and Ca (bone ash) |

At pH 6.6 with high OM, Mg is *probably* sufficient → **0–1 g/L** in the charge is plenty; excess Mg is more likely to hurt than help.

### 4.2 Job 2 — Phosphorus Recovery (Struvite)

Struvite (MgNH₄PO₄·6H₂O) is the dominant mechanism in the Mg + urine + biochar literature. It matters *specifically* on Andisols:

| Finding | Source |
|---|---|
| Epsom salt (OMRI epsomite) dosed to **1.2× molar P** removed **94.7%** of phosphate from hydrolyzed urine; 90.5% struvite; organic-certifiable | Iftikhar et al., *J. Cleaner Prod.* (2024) |
| Struvite optimum **Mg:P molar ≥1.2** (1.2–2:1); Mg:P ratio is the significant factor for P recovery | Iftikhar (2024); Barbosa et al., *Chem. Eng. J.* (2016) |
| **2:1 tested directly:** max P recovery **99%** (MgO, 2:1); 89% (MgCl₂, 2:1); increasing Mg:P up to **2.2:1** promotes P removal; P removal *linear* with Mg:P (75% at 2:1 "overdose", pH 8.2) | Barbosa et al. (2016); Song et al.; Wilsenach et al. (cited in Barbosa) |
| Mg:PO₄:NH₄ 1.6:1:1 at pH 10 → 93% P removal as ~100% struvite | Shalaby & El Rafie, *Bull. Chem. React. Eng.* (2014) |
| Wood ash itself supplies Mg and drives struvite precipitation (37% of its Mg used for struvite) | Sakthivel et al., EAWAG (2012) |
| Struvite is a **slow-release P source "particularly effective in tropical soils with high phosphorus fixation"** — 61% of P released over 10 days at neutral pH | *Environ. Sci. Pollut. Res.* Springer (2026) |
| Mg-modified biochar (2.4 g/L Mg²⁺) co-adsorbs NH₄⁺ and PO₄³⁻ as struvite; slow-release confirmed by leaching + pot trials | *Biochar* Springer (2022) |
| Struvite requires **pH ~8.5–9.5** to form (stored/hydrolyzed urine self-raises to pH ~9; fresh urine is pH ~6) | Krishnamoorthy & Tao, multiple; Sakthivel (2012) |

**Reading:** to protect urine-P from your Andisol's 83% fixation, convert it to struvite *before* it touches the soil. That needs (a) hydrolyzed/stored urine or alkaline biochar to hit pH ~8.5–9.5, and (b) enough Mg. Stoichiometry for ~500 mg/L P urine:

| Mg:P molar | Epsom salt per L urine | Result |
|---|---|---|
| 0.8:1 (= **3 g/L**) | 3 g/L | partial struvite; most P stays unprotected |
| **1.2:1** | **~4.3 g/L** | ~95% P captured as struvite — the *balance* optimum (below) |
| **2:1** | **~7.4 g/L** | max P capture (~99%) — the *reactor* optimum, not recommended for soil |

**Why 1.2:1 beats 2:1 for a living soil.** P recovery is a rising function of Mg:P up to ~2:1, but with sharply diminishing returns: 1.2:1 already captures ~95% of P (Iftikhar), and pushing to 2:1 buys only ~4% more (~99%, Barbosa) while **nearly doubling the Epsom dose** — which adds Mg-salts cost (Mg is ~75% of struvite's operating cost) *and* sulfate salinity *and* excess Mg that antagonizes K/Ca uptake (Guan 2020). "Optimum = 1.2:1" is a **cost/balance optimum**, not a P-capture maximum. (Caveat: the 1.2:1 figure isn't universal — Munch & Barr got 94% at 1.3:1 but Adnan et al. only 62% at 1.2:1, varying with Mg source/pH/mixing. We anchor to Iftikhar's OMRI Epsom-salt result, which is the organic-relevant one.)

### 4.3 Is 2:1 "excess Mg" on an Andisol?

**Yes — by definition.** Struvite is Mg:NH₄:PO₄ = 1:1:1, so each mole of P binds only one mole of Mg. At 2:1, half the added Mg cannot enter struvite and stays free in solution:

| Mg:P | Free (excess) Mg left in charge |
|---|---|
| 1.2:1 | ~3.5 mmol/L (~85 mg/L) — negligible |
| 2:1 | ~16 mmol/L (~390 mg/L) — 4.6× more, all soluble |

That ~390 mg/L is fast-release MgSO₄, not slow-release struvite — it floods the rhizosphere when applied.

**The harm is induced K/Ca deficiency, not Mg toxicity.** Mg toxicity is rare; the damage is antagonism:
- High Mg "led to Mg overabsorption and inhibited K and Ca absorption" (tomato + cucumber) — Qu et al. (2023), PMC10610338.
- "Magnesium deficiency **or excess** hinders tomato growth, potassium and calcium uptake" — Li et al., *Plant Soil Environ.* (2024).
- Elevated K *and* Mg are linked to blossom-end rot (Ca deficiency) in fruiting vegetables — MU Extension G9069.

**The Andisol makes it worse, not better:**
- Andisols have **low ECEC at field pH** (~8 cmolc/kg; ~⅓ of pH-8.2 CEC) — "limited ability to store and exchange nutrient cations such as Ca, Mg, and K" (USDA FS, ash-influenced soils). Low buffering → a Mg²⁺ pulse shifts solution ratios more easily.
- Allophane/humus **sorb divalent cations (Mg²⁺/Ca²⁺) strongly** — displacing the weaker-held monovalent K⁺ (Nanzyo, *Global Environ. Res.*).
- The Chiriquí highlands are already **low-K** (Lorenzo 2013), and K was the only nutrient correlating with any growth metric in Cerro Punta trials (Ohiaeri 2025). Excess Mg²⁺ drives K:Mg below the 0.2 threshold → *reduced K uptake*, the wrong direction on a K-scarce soil.

**Honesty check — magnitude:** the base-cation-saturation-ratio (BCSR) literature shows real soils tolerate wide Ca:Mg/K:Mg swings with **no yield effect** when sufficiency is met — e.g., a 6-year gypsum + epsom trial drove Ca:Mg far apart with zero yield change (Chaganti et al. 2021); "Mg toxicities are rare" (4R Nutrient Management 2024). So a one-off 2:1 charge won't poison the garden — it's a *wasteful tilt*: double the Epsom for only 95%→99% P capture, plus sulfate salinity, plus Mg working against K/Ca on an already low-K Andisol. **Conclusion: 1.2:1 (~4–5 g/L) is the ceiling; 2:1 is excess Mg with no upside.**

### 4.4 Does the biochar absorb the Epsom and prevent antagonism?

The charge sequence (aged urine → biochar → Epsom) raises a fair question: won't the biochar soak up the excess Mg²⁺? **Partially, but not enough — because the Mg-capture mechanism is struvite (P-limited), not cation exchange.**

Biochar *does* adsorb divalent cations — its CEC is real and divalent-selective (fresh biochar CEC 1.3–72 cmolc/kg, Domingues 2020; up to ~137 cmolc/kg for Mg-modified, Urmia 2021; "selectively draws polyvalent cations," salinity reviews). But the mass balance defeats the absorption hypothesis:

| | Cation load |
|---|---|
| Aged urine (per L) | NH₄⁺ ~417 mmol + K⁺ ~33 + Na⁺ ~108 ≈ **~530 mmol/L** |
| Biochar @ 1:1 v/v (100–250 g) | 15 cmolc/kg → **15–37 mmol**; 72 cmolc/kg → **72–180 mmol** |

Even the best biochar holds ≤⅓ of the urine's own cations — it is saturated **3–35× over** by NH₄⁺/K⁺/Na⁺ *before* the Epsom arrives (urine N ~5.8 g/L as NH₄⁺, K ~1.3 g/L, Na ~2.5 g/L). Cation competition for the same sites is documented: Na⁺/K⁺/Ca²⁺ cut NH₄⁺ adsorption by 12.7/14.6/52.5% (*Biochar* 2022), and biochar had the *lowest* capacity of four adsorbents on real urine (Tarpeh et al. 2017, *ES&T*).

**Adsorption is displacement, not removal.** Since the sites are already full, added Mg²⁺ doesn't vanish — it displaces the monovalent K⁺/NH₄⁺ off the char (divalent > monovalent). Total soluble cations are conserved (minus precipitation): Mg²⁺ goes on, K⁺ comes off — the very antagonism you're trying to avoid.

**The only true Mg sink is struvite precipitation** (Mg + PO₄ + NH₄ → insoluble struvite on the biochar surface — the dominant mechanism in every Mg-biochar + urine study: Xu et al. 2018, *J. Environ. Eng.*), and it is capped at **1 Mg : 1 P**. At 2:1, P is exhausted at 1:1 and the extra ~390 mg/L Mg stays soluble regardless of biochar.

Biochar *does* soften the impact three ways — struvite nucleation (up to the P limit), EC/salinity buffering, and slow release — but all three are already captured by staying at 1.2:1. Biochar **mitigates** the excess-Mg problem; it does not **eliminate** it.

## 5. The "3 g/L" Question, Resolved

The number appears in the cilantro recipe as "+3 g/L Epsom salt," described as a "modest, appropriate Mg dose." It is not wrong — it is *undefined by purpose*:

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| 3 g/L Epsom → Mg | **~296 mg/L** (12.2 mmol/L) |
| vs. urine's own Mg | ~50 mg/L → Epsom adds **~6×** the urine's Mg |
| vs. Mg:P molar (urine P ~500 mg/L) | **~0.8:1** — below the 1.2:1 struvite optimum |

**Conclusion:** 3 g/L is a *safe, do-no-harm middle ground* that matches the common extension rule-of-thumb (~1 tbsp/gal ≈ 3.9 g/L). It is **not** a literature-derived optimum for either Mg nutrition (which is soil-test-driven) or P recovery (which needs ~4–8 g/L). Keep it only if you want a conservative Mg maintenance dose; adjust up if you're targeting P, and drop it if your soil test shows Mg adequate.

---

## 6. Recommended Protocol

1. **Run one exchangeable-cations soil test** (Ca, Mg, K + CEC). This replaces every g/L guess with a number.
2. **Mg adequate (likely at pH 6.6):** use **0–1 g/L** Epsom in the charge — or skip it. Excess Mg antagonizes K/Ca.
3. **Mg low (<60 ppm / <0.5 cmolc/kg):** keep **~2–3 g/L** in the charge (≈300 mg/L Mg); watch for interveinal chlorosis on older leaves.
4. **For P protection on the P-fixing Andisol (the high-value use):** charge biochar with **stored/aged urine** (pH ~9) + **~4–5 g/L Epsom** (Mg:P ≈ 1.2:1). Let struvite form on the biochar surface → slow-release P + Mg product that resists allophane lock-up. Apply ≤10% v/v in the planting hole.
5. **Charging baseline (regardless of Epsom):** urine:biochar **1:1 (v/v)**, macerate ≥1 h, apply low-dose root-zone (not broadcast).
