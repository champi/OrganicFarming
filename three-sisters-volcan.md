# Three Sisters (Corn, Beans, Squash) — Direct-Seed, No-Mound, No-Bed Method for Volcán, Panama

> **September 2026. Decision record + planting plan for the Three Sisters on Volcán's Andisol slopes. The ruling choices: (1) skip mounds and raised beds — direct-seed into the soil as-is, on contour, with machete chop-and-drop site prep; (2) plant corn in clusters of 4 at ~1 m spacing in a block (the research-optimal pattern), not a 30 cm grid. This supersedes the mound-only method in `volcan-garden-bed-slope-design.md` §6 for slope sites.**

---

## 1. The decision

Three decisions were settled, in order:

1. **No mounds.** The mound's drainage role is redundant on a slope (gravity already drains it). Its nutrient-concentration role is replaced by a pinch of amendment in each cluster. Its crop-separation role is replaced by sparse squash between clusters.
2. **No raised beds.** Beds are a drainage feature for flat/low land, not an erosion feature. On a slope, direct seeding on contour controls erosion *better* than any bed.
3. **Direct seed into soil as-is.** Cut the existing wild cover with a machete (chop-and-drop) and plant straight through the wilted mat. Minimal work, minimal disturbance.

---

## 2. Why this works on a slope (evidence)

The literature is unambiguous that contour + minimal disturbance is the *correct* minimal-work system for sloped land:

- Contour planting reduced soil loss **74%** vs. up-and-down-hill planting, and **no-till** had the least erosion of any system tested (Jasa, Dickey & Shelton 1986; RBCS 2013). No-till left ~60% less soil loss than disk tillage.
- A no-till/minimum-till contour strip system on **10–12% slopes** in Kentucky showed **no appreciable erosion through an 80-inch rain year** (UK/SCS demonstration, Wells et al.).
- Maize was more effective at controlling soil loss than soybean regardless of seeding direction (RBCS 2013) — corn is itself an erosion-control crop.

The mound in traditional Three Sisters exists for three reasons (Real Self-Sufficiency):

| Mound function | How it's covered here (no mound) |
|---|---|
| **Drainage** for corn (susceptible to root rot in waterlogged soil) | The slope already drains by gravity. Keep corn on the draining slope, not on flat benches. |
| **Concentrated nutrients** in a defined zone | A pinch of bokashi/compost + bone ash + AMF in each cluster (§9). |
| **Separate corn from sprawling squash** | Squash goes sparse, in the gaps between clusters (§8). |

---

## 3. Site prep — machete chop-and-drop

Cut the existing wild cover with a **machete** where corn will be sown. Do not dig or hoe — the design doc's core rule is "disturbance = erosion."

- **Cut low**, plus a **~30 cm margin** around the planting block so seedlings get light. Don't scalp just the seed hole — corn seedlings are shaded out by standing cover next to them.
- **Leave the cut material in place** as mulch. That is simultaneously erosion control, moisture retention, and weed suppression — the three things a slope needs, for free.
- **Let it wilt 2–4 days** before sowing if thick/green, then dibble corn straight through the mat.

**Flowering timing:**

- **Flowering but no mature seed yet → ideal.** Cut *now*. Annuals cut at flowering mostly won't regrow, and cutting before seed set avoids dumping a fresh weed-seed bank.
- **Already set mature seed → fine, expect a flush.** Re-cut the seedling flush once, then let the squash canopy smother the rest.
- **Perennial clumping grasses** will resprout from the crown after a machete cut. Re-cut the lanes a couple of times until squash closes canopy, or smother with cardboard + cut grass where a one-time kill is wanted.

## 4. Seed math and planting pattern

From measured seed weight: **30 seeds = 16 g** → 0.533 g/seed.

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Total seed | 5 lb = 2,268 g |
| Total seeds | ≈ **4,252** |
| Seeds per cluster | 8 (2 seeds × 4 spots, thin to 4 strongest corn) |
| Clusters | ≈ **531** |
| Cluster spacing | **~1 m (3–4 ft)**, block layout |

### The pattern (research-optimal, corrected)

Corn in **clusters ("hills") of 4**, clusters **~1 m apart**, arranged in a **block** (≥4×4 clusters). A "hill" here is a **flat, hoe-loosened circle — not a raised mound** (Carrick Seeds: *"my hills are no higher than the surrounding ground"*).

The earlier 30 cm grid (11 plants/m²) was **too dense**. The peer-reviewed optimum (Mt. Pleasant & Burt 2010; Mt. Pleasant 2016) is **~4–5 corn plants/m²**, achieved by 4 corn per 1 m cluster:

- Corn yield **increased** with closer spacing and higher density, peaking at 20,000 plants/acre (~5/m²) and 40" (~1 m) mound spacing.
- Corn yield was **barely affected** by the beans and squash, but beans yielded 6–10× less and squash up to 20× less in polyculture. **Corn is the anchor; beans and squash are the bonus.**

```
  o  o  o  o  o      o = corn cluster (4 corn), ~1 m apart
  o  o  o  o  o
  o  o  o  o  o      b = beans climb each corn stalk
  o  o  o  o  o      S = squash, 1 per ~4–8 clusters (sparse)
  S        S
```

- Corn is wind-pollinated and needs a **block, not a single row**; minimum **4×4 clusters (16 plants)** for reliable ear fill (Seed-Bank.ca; Real Self-Sufficiency). A square block out-pollinates a long rectangle of the same count.
- Contour slope-length limits (OMAFRA): 1–2% slope → max 120 m; 3–5% → 90 m; 6–8% → 60 m.
- **Scale:** 531 clusters × ~1 m² = **~530 m² of corn**. 5 lb remains more than a family plot needs — plant a fraction and keep the rest as reserve.

## 5. Planting sequence and timing

The stagger is the single most common point of failure — do **not** plant everything at once.

| Step | Timing | Action |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Corn | Week 1 | Per cluster: sow 2 seeds at each of 4 spots (~8 seeds), ~2–3 cm deep; thin to 4 strongest. Clusters ~1 m apart in a block on contour. |
| 2. Beans | ~2–3 weeks later (corn 10–15 cm tall) | Sow 1–2 climbing bean seeds per corn stalk (4–6 per cluster). |
| 3. Squash | ~1 week after beans | Sow vining squash, **1 plant per 4–8 corn clusters**, in the gaps/edges. |

Plant beans at the same time as corn and they will overtake and pull the young stalks down. Corn planted too shallow grows weak roots and topples once beans climb it — plant **deeper rather than shallower** (Cornell; Harvest Savvy).

---

## 6. Corn

- **Variety:** sturdy flour/dent corn, not short popcorn — highland Volcán is windy, and beans add load. Cornell and UAEX recommend dent/flint/flour corns for Three Sisters.
- **Density:** ~4–5 plants/m² (clusters of 4 at ~1 m). Denser than this weakens stalks and increases P competition on Andisol with no corn-yield benefit; sparser loses wind pollination.
- **Wind/rain lodging:** once established, bean vines *stabilize* maize in wind. On exposed sites, stake the outside clusters or accept a few lodgers.
- **Don't count on beans for this year's N** — fixed N mostly releases next season (UAEX). Feed the corn now via in-cluster amendment (§9).

---

## 7. Beans

- **Climbing/pole beans only** (Genuine Cornfield, Scarlet Runner, Kentucky Wonder, Cherokee Trail of Tears). Bush beans won't climb and don't serve the system.
- 1–2 seeds per corn stalk (4–6 per cluster), sown when corn is 10–15 cm tall. Thin to the strongest.
- Beans' N fixation is not the point on Andisol — the soil is **P-limited, not N-limited** (§9). Expect bean yield to be sharply lower than a bean monoculture (Mt. Pleasant & Burt 2010) — they're the bonus, not the main crop.

---

## 8. Squash

- **Vining winter squash only** (butternut, Hubbard, Connecticut Field pumpkin, etc.). Bush types don't sprawl, so they provide no living mulch or pest deterrence (Real Self-Sufficiency).
- **1 plant per 4–8 corn clusters** (Carrick Seeds; Zhang et al. 2014 planted squash every other row). This is far sparser than a row/lane fill — squash is a space hog and yields up to 20× less when crowded in with corn.
- Squash is the erosion-control and living-mulch "sister" — its canopy is what protects the slope during the wet season.

---

## 9. Nutrient management on Andisol (the P problem)

Andisols fix phosphorus via amorphous Fe/Al and allophane — Chiriquí retains up to **83% of applied P** (Morán et al. 2022). Beans' N fixation is irrelevant to this; corn is P-hungry.

The documented fix, applied in each cluster at planting:

- **Native AMF inoculation** — raised maize P uptake and shoot growth to **80–103% of full chemical-fertilizer control** on Andisols (Cahyani et al. 2023, 2024; Borie et al. 2019).
- **Organic matter + P-solubilizing microbes + bone ash** (Masdariah et al. 2019: mycorrhiza + organic matter raised P-available up to 112% on Andisol).
- **Bokashi / gallinaza / compost** as the organic-matter carrier.

**Caution — keep amendment off the seed.** Concentrated fertilizer placed directly in the seed hole cut germination 50–67% on a volcanic ash soil (co-situs spot application study, Japan). Put the amendment **2–3 cm below or beside** the seed, never touching it.

If the soil pH is < 5.5, consider a small lime/dolomite band at planting (Cargua-Chávez et al. 2025: 1.0 t/ha dolomite raised Andisol pH above the Al³⁺-toxicity threshold for corn).

### AMF seed coating (the practical how — Great White)

A commercial mycorrhizal product (e.g., **Great White**) fits the whole trio because **corn, beans, and squash are all endomycorrhizal (AMF) hosts** — one powder covers all three. Great White's active mycorrhizae are all endo species: *Glomus aggregatum, intraradices, mosseae, etunicatum* at 83 propagules/g each, plus *Glomus clarum, monosporum, deserticola, Paraglomus brasilianum, Gigaspora margarita* at 11 propagules/g each — **≈387 endomycorrhizal propagules/g total**. (The label also lists ectomycorrhizae — *Pisolithus tinctorius* 187,875/g, *Rhizopogon*, *Scleroderma* — but those are tree fungi and irrelevant to any of the three sisters.)

- **Species fit:** *Glomus intraradices* (= *Rhizophagus irregularis*) is the best-tested colonizer of **both corn and squash** — 87% corn / 95% squash root colonization and the highest tissue P in a head-to-head trial (Frontiers in Agronomy 2022). *G. mosseae* and *G. etunicatum* are also proven on maize.
- **Why it matters in polyculture:** a shared **common mycorrhizal network (CMN)** physically links bean and maize roots and transfers bean-fixed N to the corn (milpa review, Comptes Rendus Biologies 2024). Inoculating gives that inter-plant network a head start — this is the strongest reason to use AMF in the Three Sisters specifically, beyond per-plant benefit.
- **Beans still need a separate rhizobium inoculant.** Great White has *Bacillus* / *Paenibacillus* / *Pseudomonas* / *Azotobacter* but **no rhizobia**; coat bean seed with Great White + a legume inoculant together (compatible).

**Rate (corrected):** Great White's corn/bean/squash-relevant density is only ~387 propagules/g, so **1 teaspoon per 5 lb is too thin** — it works out to ~0.3 endo propagules/seed across ~4,250 seeds, well below the ~3–20 propagules/seed used in successful AMF seed-coating trials (Rocha et al. 2019; EndoFuse corn trials). Use **2 teaspoons minimum, 1 full tablespoon preferred, per 5 lb**. Spores don't have to be glued to the seed coat — dust generously **into the cluster/hole** and the excess powder in the furrow still colonizes the emerging roots.

**Bentonite-carrier method (chosen — spread then mist):**

Bentonite is a recognized, inert AMF carrier (Ghorui et al. 2025 lists bentonite alongside talc/kaolin/vermiculite; US Patent 5300127 lists clay; MycoApply Endo/Ecto ships as 99.94% clay carrier). It won't harm the spores, and Russian field work found bentonite/montmorillonite pre-sowing treatment *increased* corn yield (Khalikov et al. 2025; Abubikerov et al. 2025 — the exact field rate is paywalled).

- **Ratio:** **3 tablespoons bentonite to 2 teaspoons Great White** spreads evenly. Bentonite is only the *spreader* — it adds no spores and never reduces how much Great White you need; total Great White is still the limiting factor.
- **Method:** toss seed + dry powder (Great White + bentonite) in a clean bucket until evenly dusted, **then** mist lightly with **non-chlorinated water** (rain/well water, or tap water left overnight) and stir again so it adheres. Optionally add a **drop of molasses/honey** to the mist as a sticker.
- **Light mist only.** Bentonite is a swelling clay — a heavy spray gels into clumps. You want the powder just damp enough to stick.
- **Plant the same day.** Water activates the spores; wet-coated seed won't store. Don't pre-mix Great White + bentonite and store it — mix fresh at planting.
- **Do not** use fungicide-dusted seed or chlorinated water — both kill the spores.
- **This is a starter inoculation, not the whole P fix.** AMF is one half; the other half is the organic-matter carrier (bokashi/bone ash) in the cluster. AMF alone on a P-fixing soil is doing half the job.

---

## 10. Labor reality check

Three Sisters **costs more labor, not less**: a peer-reviewed comparison (Cryan et al. 2024) found **lower yield per labor-hour than monoculture**, mostly from managing the climbing beans and harvest access. You are *not* doing this to save work — you're doing it for polyculture resilience, living ground cover (erosion control), and a complete diet per unit land. Corn is the real crop; beans and squash are the bonus and the living mulch.
