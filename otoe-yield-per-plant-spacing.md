# Otoe (*Xanthosoma*): Yield per Plant and 1 m × 1 m Spacing — Worldwide Literature

> **Literature review, Nov 2026.** How much one otoe plant (tannia, yautía, tiquisque, quequisque, macabo, malanga in Cuba) yields, and whether 1 m between rows × 1 m between plants (150 plants on 150 m²) is a good layout for Volcán.
> Growing method: [otoe-high-yield-organic-volcan.md](otoe-high-yield-organic-volcan.md). Feeding per plant: [otoe-nutrition-per-plant-volcan.md](otoe-nutrition-per-plant-volcan.md).

## 1. Short answer

- **Yes, 1 m × 1 m works.** It is the most common spacing in the world for otoe grown alone (Pacific, Venezuela, Cameroon highlands, Saint Lucia smallholders). 150 m² = 150 plants.
- **Per plant, edible cormels, 12-month crop:**
  - Typical farm: **0.3–0.8 kg**
  - Well-managed trial: **1–2 kg**
  - Best recorded: **3.5 kg**, from improved clones on heavily fertilized, high-organic-matter soil, harvested at 15 months (Costa Rica).
- **Realistic for 150 plants in Volcán at 12 months: ~100–200 kg of cormels** (0.7–1.3 kg per plant). Volcán is cooler than every high-yield trial site, so this is an estimate.
- **A second year in the ground can double or triple it.** In Puerto Rico, Morada gave ~0.8 kg of tubers per plant at 12 months and ~2 kg at 24 months, plus ~3 kg of corm.
- **The trade-off:** 1 × 1 m gives the most per plant and the biggest cormels. Closer spacing (1 × 0.5 m) gives **20–60% more per m²**, but less per plant. The earlier 0.75 × 0.75 m suggestion in [otoe-high-yield-organic-volcan.md](otoe-high-yield-organic-volcan.md) is the middle ground.

**Pattern:** cormel number (5 to 16) and cormel size (50 to 150 g) set the yield. The highest per-plant yields all came from four things together: a vigorous clone, lots of K and N, steady water, and a crop left 14–24 months.

## 3. Spacing: what trials found

| Trial | Spacings | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Ghana (Safo-Kantanka) | 1 × 1, 1 × 0.75, 1 × 0.5 m | Cormel yield per area **+18% and +60%** over 1 × 1 m. Cormels per plant unchanged, cormels **smaller** at 1 × 0.5 m. Per plant, 1 × 0.5 m ≈ 80% of 1 × 1 m |
| Ghana (Hotsonyame & Ahiekpor 1994) | 1.23, 1.78, 2.78 plants/m² | Marketable cormels per area **+19% and +51%** at higher density. Weight and number per plant fell, cormel size did not. Square vs rectangle vs triangle: no difference |
| India (Ghosh) | 1 × 1, 1 × 0.75, 1 × 0.5 m | No significant difference; 1 × 0.5 m tended to be highest |
| Indonesia (Arumsari & Suwarto 2018) | 1 × 1 vs 1 × 0.5 m | 1 × 1 m: more cormels per plant and +157 g fresh cormels per plant (5-month crop only) |
| Puerto Rico mountains (Abruña 1967) | several populations | More plants = more yield in a **dry** year; **no effect** in a wet year with higher yields |
| Nigeria (Orji et al. 2018) | 1 × 0.3/0.4/0.5 m | No effect: root rot blight hid every treatment |
| Kay 1987 (review) | 0.6–1.8 m | 1 × 1 m most common (~1 t/ha of planting material). Wider = more per plant but more weeding; 1.5 × 1.5 m reported best per ha in one case |

**Spacings recommended by country:** Panama MIDA (otoe blanco) 1 m × 0.45–0.75 m. Costa Rica MAG 1.5 m ridges × 0.4 m. Cuba 0.9 × 0.35–0.4 m. Nicaragua INTA 0.81 × 0.61 m. Puerto Rico 0.91 × 0.61 m. Venezuela (Monagas farmers) 1 × 1 m most often. Cameroon (Dschang) Xanthosoma 1 × 1 m. Saint Lucia 1.3 m × 0.6–0.75 m commercially, 1 × 1 or 1.3 × 1.3 m on small farms. Commercial guides pack plants closer to raise yield per ha. Home and small-farm practice stays near 1 × 1 m.

## 4. What this means for 150 plants at 1 × 1 m in Volcán

- **Good reasons for 1 × 1 m:** bigger cormels (better for selling), room to earth up and walk, more air between plants in the wet season (less leaf disease and *Pythium*), and room to harvest cormels from the side while the plant keeps growing.
- **Cost:** about 20–60% less per m² than 1 × 0.5 m, and bare soil for the first ~4 months (weeds hurt most in the first 3–5.5 months). Fill the gap: CARDI growers sow cowpea, okra, pigeon pea or maize between tannia rows **after** the tannia is up. Short crops (beans, radish, lettuce) in the middle of the 1 m row, harvested before month 4, fit best. My inference: no trial tested this in Volcán.
- **Cool-climate caveat:** otoe wants a mean of 20–25 °C. Volcán (~18 °C) is at the cold edge: Costa Rica's manual gives 18 °C as the minimum. Still, highland trials in Cameroon (1,600 m) gave 0.8–1.5 kg per plant, and PNG farmers grow it up to ~2,000 m. Expect slower growth. Plan on a 12–15 month crop, not 9–10.
- **Harvest strategy:** tannia does not need to be dug all at once. Cormels can be harvested over 500+ days by pulling soil back, taking the big cormels and covering the plant again. Kay reports that plants can crop this way for ~6 years before vigour declines. For family use plus small sales, harvest the biggest cormels from month ~10, and let the plants go on.
- **Expected harvest, 150 plants:**

| Scenario | kg/plant | 150 plants |
|---|---|---|
| Poor (cold, root rot, little feeding) | 0.3 | ~45 kg |
| Realistic Volcán, 12 months | 0.7–1.3 | ~100–200 kg |
| Very good, 15–24 months | 2 | ~300 kg |
| Record (trial clones, 15 months) | 3.5 | ~525 kg |

## 5. Gaps

- No per-plant yield trial for otoe at ~1,400 m and ~18 °C. The Volcán numbers are extrapolated from Cameroon (1,600 m) and Ethiopia (~1,700 m).
- No published MIDA/IDIAP per-plant data for Panamanian otoe (San Andrés). A press report gave ~200 qq/ha in a good year (~9 t/ha).
- The record yields came from in-vitro (virus-free) clones that were imported. Local planting material may yield less.
