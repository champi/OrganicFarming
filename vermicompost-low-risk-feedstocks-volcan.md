# Lowest-Risk Worm Feedstocks in Tierras Altas — Avoiding Agrochemical Residues

> Compiled from a worldwide literature review (Oct 2026) on whether vermicast made from conventional-farm waste
> (Cerro Punta) is safe for organic use.
> Focus: red wrigglers (*Eisenia fetida*), Volcán / Tierras Altas, Chiriquí, Panama.

---

## 1. Why feedstock choice matters

- Cerro Punta growers use **36 pesticides**. Soil tests found **24 active ingredients** (77 detections across 5 farms), including chlorpyrifos, cypermethrin, chlorothalonil, triazole fungicides, and legacy **DDT/DDD/DDE** (Herrera et al. 2021).
- Most current-use insecticides break down during vermicomposting. **Triazole and SDHI fungicides, organochlorines, copper and the picloram family of herbicides do not.**
- What the worms eat is the *waste* part: peels, outer leaves, culls and attached soil. It carries more residue than the washed, peeled produce that residue rankings are based on.

## 2. Lowest-risk list (use these first)

| # | Feedstock | Why it's low risk | Notes for the worm bin |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Forest leaf litter, unsprayed grass, garden trimmings from your own land** | Unsprayed, so you know where it came from. | Ideal bedding. Grass only if no herbicide was used on it. |
| 2 | **Banana / plantain** (leaves, pseudostems, peels) from backyards or smallholders | EWG Clean Fifteen; local plants are usually unsprayed. | Excellent feed. Avoid waste from commercial plantations, which spray fungicides heavily. |
| 3 | **Coffee pulp** from organic or low-input farms | Plant material, low spray where no rust program is used. | Ask whether copper or triazoles are sprayed for rust. Acidic, so mix it with bedding. |
| 4 | **Bakery waste** (stale bread, flour sweepings) | Baking dilutes and heats the ingredients, so residues should be low. Possible stored-grain insecticides or glyphosate traces (not measured). | Keep it to **10–20% of the feed**. Soak and bury it. Avoid salty, sugary or fatty products, which attract mold, mites and rodents. |
| 5 | **Sweet corn** (husks, stalks) | Top of the Clean Fifteen; few insecticides reach the husk. | Stalks are slow; chop them. Residues are mainly herbicide. |
| 6 | **Onion and leek tops** | Clean Fifteen. Mostly herbicide chemistry, and worms speed up oxyfluorfen breakdown 1.4× (Delgado-Moreno 2020). | Feed in moderation. |
| 7 | **Carrot tops and cull carrots** | Clean Fifteen; fewer sprays than other Cerro Punta crops. | Shake the soil off first. |
| 8 | **Avocado and papaya culls / pulp** | Clean Fifteen, but those samples were tested *peeled*. | Use pulp and culls more than peels. Avocado only in small amounts (fatty). |
| 9 | **Asparagus, sweet peas** | Clean Fifteen. | — |
| 10 | **Brewers' spent grain** (bagazo de cervecería) | Low *absolute* risk because malting barley is lightly sprayed. **But** about 53% of barley residues end up in the spent grain, and oil-soluble fungicides like tebuconazole stay at roughly the original barley level. | Good feed (Saba et al. 2019). It heats up and sours fast, so mix it ≥1:1 with bedding and feed in thin layers. |
| 11 | **Horse manure** — *only if it passes a bean or tomato bioassay* and the hay and pasture weren't treated with picloram (Torban) | Good feed once aged or pre-composted. | Hot composting or "cooking" does **not** destroy picloram or clopyralid. Never use it without the bioassay. |

## 3. Avoid

| Feedstock | Reason |
|---|---|
| **Potato** (peels, culls, haulm) | Up to **17 fungicide sprays per cycle** (mancozeb, chlorothalonil, copper); potato is on the 2025 Dirty Dozen. |
| **Strawberries** | No. 2 on the Dirty Dozen. |
| **Lettuce, celery, parsley, spinach, kale** | Leafy, lots of surface area, short gap between the last spray and harvest. |
| **Tomato, tree tomato, peppers** | Frequent fungicides and insecticides, including **abamectin**, which is very toxic to worms. |
| **Cabbage, broccoli outer leaves** (conventional) | Calendar spraying against diamondback moth. Use only from IPM growers. |
| **Citrus peel** | Post-harvest fungicide coatings (general knowledge, not sourced). |
| **Pasture cattle/horse manure or grass of unknown history** | Picloram damages crops at about 0.3 ppb, below what labs can detect (20–50 ppb). |
| **Untreated *gallinaza* from industrial farms** | Not permitted under RTCA 67.06.74:16 ("agricultura industrial"). |
| **Anything with soil attached** from Cerro Punta fields | Soil carries DDT residues and copper. |

---

## 4. Rules for any feedstock

1. **Ask the grower:** when was the last spray (wait at least 2–4 weeks), and was anything systemic or persistent used (triazoles, imidacloprid, abamectin, picloram)?
2. **Hot pre-compost** conventional material before feeding it to the worms (55–77 °C for at least 3 days, turned at least 5 times in 15 days, which matches RTCA). Then cure the vermicompost for at least 4 months.
3. **Mix in clean material** from section 2, items 1–3.
4. **Watch the colony.** Worms that die, lose weight or try to escape mean the feed is contaminated.
5. **Bioassay every batch:** sow beans, peas or tomato in the vermicast next to a clean mix. Cupped or twisted leaves mean a persistent herbicide is present.
6. **Lab-test the first batches:** MIDA's Laboratorio de Residuos de Plaguicidas (GC-MS/MS) for pesticides, plus Cu, Zn and Cd. Use the EU organic limits as a benchmark: **Cu 70, Zn 200, Cd 0.7 mg/kg dry matter**.
7. **For certified sales:** RTCA §5.1.5(d) requires the certifier to review non-organic inputs. Get approval first and keep records of where each feedstock came from.
